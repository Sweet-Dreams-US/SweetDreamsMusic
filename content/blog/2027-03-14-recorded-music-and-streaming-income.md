You release a song, and people stream it. Money follows, but it does not arrive in one payment from one place. Streaming income for recorded music is split into several streams, each paid by a different organization to a different person.

This article explains the main parts, so that an independent or signed artist knows what to register and what to ask about.

## One song, two kinds of copyright

A recorded song holds two separate things. The first is the musical work, which is the composition: the melody and the lyrics. The second is the sound recording, which is the finished track that was recorded and released.

These are separate copyrights and they can be owned by different people. A writer may own the composition while a label or the artist owns the recording. Each one earns income when the song is streamed, and each is handled by its own system. Knowing which part you own is the first step toward getting paid on it.

## Income from the composition

When a song streams on a service in the United States, the composition earns what is called a mechanical royalty. The Music Modernization Act of 2018 set up how those royalties are licensed and collected.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that Title I of the Act created a blanket licensing system that lets digital music providers make and distribute digital phonorecord deliveries, which include permanent downloads, limited downloads and interactive streams. The Office designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, to collect and distribute the mechanical royalties under that license.

:::note Worth knowing
The MLC says that each month, digital services send it usage data and royalties for music streamed and downloaded. It matches that usage to songs registered by its members, works out what is owed, and distributes royalties monthly. It reports over $4 billion in streaming royalties distributed. Membership is free.
:::

For a songwriter, the practical point is registration. The Copyright Office notes that songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive payment under the blanket license. If you wrote a song that streams, check whether your songs are registered.

## Income from the recording

The recording earns money in a different way. Berklee Online's music business course lists revenue from the digital transmission performance right of a sound recording as a core topic, and it covers interactive, on demand streaming revenue separately from non interactive webcasting and satellite radio.

In plain terms, a listener who picks a specific song on a service is using interactive streaming. A listener who hears a station style stream is using non interactive service. They are paid under different rules, which is why a single song can show up in several different statements.

:::note Worth knowing
Berklee Online describes featured artists and producers as earning through advances and royalties. Its course description also says that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists, so streaming is one part of an artist's income and not the whole of it.
:::

## Producers, mixers and engineers

Streaming income is not only for the person on the cover art. Title III of the Music Modernization Act, called the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets music producers, mixers and sound engineers receive a share of royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. The Copyright Office says it establishes a process for the designated collective, SoundExchange, to distribute those royalties under a letter of direction.

If you work on records in any of those roles, or you pay someone who does, it is worth finding out how a letter of direction works for your project. The Copyright Office page is a good starting point.

## What to do before and after a release

A release plan is a plan for income as well as for promotion. A short list covers most of it.

Decide who owns the composition and who owns the recording, and write it down. If several people wrote the song, agree on the split before the release and not after.

Register the songs you wrote with The MLC so that mechanical royalties from streaming have somewhere to go. Registration is free for members.

Make sure the recording is set up to be paid correctly through the system that handles digital performance of sound recordings, and ask the person distributing your music how that is handled for you.

Keep records of everything. When statements arrive from different organizations, a clear list of songs, owners and dates makes it much easier to find what is missing.

Last, treat streaming as one source among several. Berklee Online's course description frames an artist's strategy as a mix of live performance, branding, copyrights and recorded music income.
