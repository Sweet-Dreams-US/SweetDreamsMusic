When you finish a song in the studio, you usually think of one thing you made. The law sees two. There is the song itself, and there is the recording of it. Each one has its own author, its own owner and its own income. This post is about the second one: the recording, and who counts as its author.

## Two works in one song

Copyright treats the music and lyrics as one work, called a musical work. A recording of an artist performing that song is a separate work, called a sound recording. The U.S. Copyright Office puts it plainly: the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself, and it does not cover the music, lyrics or other content that the recording carries.

That split explains a lot of confusion. A songwriter can own the song and never touch the recording. A performer can own the recording of a song someone else wrote. When people say "who owns this track," the honest answer starts with another question: which of the two works do you mean?

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office defines a sound recording as a work separate from the underlying music and lyrics. Its copyright covers the recording itself, not the song embodied in it.
:::

## Who counts as the author

The Copyright Office's guide to registering sound recordings names two kinds of author. The first is the performer featured in the recording. The second is the producer who captured and processed the sounds that appear in the final recording.

Notice what this says. The author of a recording is not only the artist whose name is on the cover. The person who captured and shaped the sound can be an author too. In many sessions the artist and the producer are the same person, and then the question is simple. When they are different people, it is worth deciding early how the two roles are treated.

The same guide gives an example of two people who co-create five unpublished sound recordings and co-own the copyright. Shared authorship is a normal outcome. It is not a problem to be avoided, but it does mean decisions about the recording involve more than one person.

## When the recording is a work made for hire

Sometimes the author is not the person who made the sounds. The Copyright Office describes two situations where a recording is a work made for hire.

In the first, the performer or producer made the recording as part of a typical employment relationship. The employer is then the author. In the second, the performer or producer made the recording for a third party as a contribution to a collective work, and the parties agreed in writing that it is a work made for hire. The third party is then the author.

Work made for hire is its own area of copyright law, and the Copyright Office has a separate circular on it. This post does not cover every rule. The point for an artist is narrower: the way a session is set up can change who the author is.

## Why a written agreement matters

The work made for hire rule above depends on a written agreement in the commissioned case. That is one reason to put terms on paper before a session, not after the mix is done.

A short written agreement can say who the performers and producers are, who is treated as the author, who owns the master, and how credits will read. It can also say whether anyone is working for hire. None of this requires a long document. It requires that everyone agrees before the recording exists, while no one has a reason to disagree.

If you are unsure what to put in an agreement, a music attorney is the right person to ask. This post is general information and not legal advice.

## What authorship means for money

Authorship matters because income follows ownership, and the two works earn in different ways. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams treats them separately. For compositions, it covers income from public performance and mechanical reproduction. For master recordings, it covers income from digital transmission and from licensing recordings for film, television, commercials and video games.

The Music Modernization Act shows the same split in practice. Its Title I created the Mechanical Licensing Collective, which collects and distributes mechanical royalties for musical works. Songwriters and publishers register with it to be paid. That system is about the song, not the recording.

Title III of the same law is about recordings. It lets music producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings, through a process in which SoundExchange distributes the money under a letter of direction. So the people who helped make a recording can have a path to being paid, but only if they are set up to receive it.

:::note Worth knowing
Under the Music Modernization Act, the Mechanical Licensing Collective handles royalties for musical works. Royalties for producers, mixers and engineers on sound recordings are handled through SoundExchange.
:::

## Registration and protection

A recording does not need to be registered or published to be protected. The Copyright Office states that neither registration nor publication is required for protection under current law.

Registration is still a separate step you can take. To register a claim in a sound recording, you submit a completed application, a nonrefundable filing fee and a copy of the work. When you fill out the application, the author question comes up, which is another reason to settle it before you start.

## What to do before your next session

Before you record, write down four things: who is performing, who is producing, who will be listed as the author of the recording, and whether anyone is working for hire. Ask each person to confirm in writing. Keep the file with your project.

Then keep the song and the recording separate in your head and in your paperwork. The song has its owners and its income. The recording has its own.
