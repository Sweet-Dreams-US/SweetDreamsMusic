Most artists know the recording side of music: the studio, the mix, the release date. Publishing is the other half. It is the business side of the song itself, and it decides who gets paid when the song is played, streamed, copied or placed in a video. This article explains what publishing is in plain terms so you can make better choices before you release.

## One song, two copyrights

A recorded song carries two separate copyrights. The U.S. Copyright Office describes the first as the musical work, which is the underlying composition and lyrics. Think of the notes on sheet music. The second is the sound recording, which is the recorded audio made by performers, producers and others.

Songwriters, meaning composers and lyricists, earn from the musical work. They do not earn from the sound recording copyright as songwriters. Publishing deals with the first of these, the song as written.

## What a publisher does

A publisher looks after the musical work. In the traditional arrangement, the Copyright Office explains, the songwriter assigns the copyright to a publisher. The publisher pays the songwriter's share and keeps the publisher's share as its compensation. In return the publisher licenses the work, registers it, monitors how it is used and collects royalties.

There are other setups. In co-publishing, the songwriter keeps part of the copyright and also keeps a portion of the publisher's share. With administration, an administrator handles registration and collection for a fee, usually a percentage of royalties, without taking ownership. A songwriter can also self-administer and do these tasks personally.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office lists four ways publishing work can be arranged: traditional publishing, co-publishing, administration, and self-administration. They differ mainly in who owns the copyright and who keeps what share.
:::

## Performance royalties

When a song is performed publicly, the musical work earns a performance royalty. Public performance includes radio, television, streaming, bars and arenas. Songwriters and publishers can register with a performing rights organization, or PRO. The Copyright Office names ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR. PROs license these performances and collect the money.

The Copyright Office notes that PROs typically pay songwriters and publishers their royalties directly, rather than routing payment through the publisher. Their licenses are non-exclusive, so a copyright owner can still license performances directly.

## Mechanical royalties

A mechanical royalty is paid when a musical work is reproduced and distributed. The Copyright Office explains that the compulsory license in section 115 of the Copyright Act allows others to reproduce and distribute musical works in audio-only form, and that songwriters generally cannot refuse it.

For digital uses, such as interactive streams and downloads, the Music Modernization Act changed how this works. The Copyright Office says Title I replaced the song-by-song system with a blanket licensing system for digital music providers. On July 8, 2019, it designated Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. as the Mechanical Licensing Coordinator. The Mechanical Licensing Collective, or MLC, collects and distributes digital mechanical royalties.

To be paid by the MLC, you need to register with it. The Copyright Office says the MLC must match your songs to recordings and needs your payment and tax information. For physical products like CDs and vinyl, you must appear in the Copyright Office's public records, and labels or agents such as the Harry Fox Agency often handle clearance.

## Synchronization licenses

When a song is used in a film, TV show, advertisement or video game, the composition needs a separate synchronization license. The Copyright Office points out that there is no compulsory option for sync. The rights holders negotiate directly, so fees are set by negotiation, not by statute.

Berklee Online lists the same revenue areas in its music business course: the performance right of a composition, the mechanical reproduction right, and synchronization, along with copyright licensing and ownership and how they turn into revenue. These are the main streams that publishing covers.

## Why registration matters before release

The royalties above do not collect themselves. A PRO pays songwriters and publishers who have registered with it. The MLC needs your songs registered and matched to recordings, plus your payment and tax information. If a song goes out unregistered, the money can sit unclaimed until the paperwork is done.

A short checklist before you release:

1. Know who wrote the song and what share each writer owns.
2. Decide whether you will sign with a publisher, use an administrator, or self-administer.
3. Register with a PRO.
4. Register with the MLC for digital mechanical royalties.
5. Keep the song titles, writer names and recording details consistent everywhere you submit them.

## A note on beats and leased music

If you build a song on a beat you leased from a producer, ask how the writing credit and ownership will be handled before you release. The Copyright Office describes who owns the musical work as the basis for who earns from it, so the producer's license terms matter. This is a question for the producer or a music attorney, since the answer depends on the terms of the license.

## What to remember

Publishing is the business of the song as written. A publisher, an administrator or you yourself can handle it. Performance royalties come through PROs, digital mechanical royalties come through the MLC, and sync fees are negotiated. Register early, keep records clean, and the money has somewhere to go when your music starts to play.
