If you write songs, you own more than one kind of right in them. One of them is the mechanical right, which covers copying a song and distributing those copies. A mechanical license is the permission that goes with it.

This article explains what a mechanical license is, how the rules for digital music changed, and what an independent artist can do about it.

## Songs and recordings are two different things

Every released track holds two separate works. One is the composition, which is the song itself: the melody and the lyrics. The other is the sound recording, which is one particular recorded performance of that song.

Different people can own each one. If you write and record your own song, you may own both. If you record someone else's song, you own at most the recording, and the writer or their publisher owns the composition.

Mechanical licenses belong to the composition side. They are about the song, not about your recording of it.

## What a mechanical license is

A mechanical license gives someone permission to reproduce and distribute a song. The word "mechanical" is old. It comes from the days when copies of songs were physical, such as records and tapes. Under the Music Modernization Act it also covers digital downloads and interactive streams.

Berklee Online lists the mechanical reproduction right as one of the composition rights, next to the performance right and the derivative right. Each one can earn money in its own way. When a song is reproduced and distributed under a mechanical license, the owner of the composition is entitled to a payment, usually called a mechanical royalty.

A simple example: an artist records a cover of another writer's song and releases it. The release reproduces and distributes the writer's composition, so the writer is owed a mechanical royalty. A license is how that gets sorted out.

## How the rules changed for digital music

For a long time, licensing a song for a digital release meant dealing with it one song at a time. The U.S. Copyright Office describes the Music Modernization Act as replacing the existing song-by-song compulsory licensing structure for making and distributing musical works.

In its place, Title I of the act created a blanket licensing system for digital music providers. The Copyright Office says this covers digital phonorecord deliveries, which it lists as permanent downloads, limited downloads and interactive streams.

:::note The key facts
The Copyright Office reports that on July 8, 2019 it named Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. as the designated Mechanical Licensing Coordinator under Title I of the Music Modernization Act.
:::

The act was signed in October 2018, according to the Mechanical Licensing Collective. It also says the act gave the Collective exclusive authority to administer the new blanket mechanical license for digital mechanical royalties.

## What the Mechanical Licensing Collective does

The Mechanical Licensing Collective, often called the MLC, administers the blanket license for digital mechanical royalties. By its own account it began operations on January 1, 2021.

The Collective collects digital mechanical royalties from digital services such as Spotify, Apple Music and Pandora, and it is set up to pay the owners of the compositions. Some of that money cannot be matched to an owner. The MLC reported in 2021 that it had received over $424 million in accrued unmatched royalties from digital services by February 16 of that year.

That number is worth taking seriously. It shows how much money can wait for writers who have not registered their songs.

## What an independent songwriter should do

The MLC says that connecting with it is free and open to independent songwriters, music publishers, administrators, collective management organizations and anyone looking to manage their song data. It says rights holders should register their works through the MLC website so they can be paid.

Here is a plain checklist:

1. Work out who owns the composition for each song you have released. If you wrote it alone, that is you. If you co-wrote it, find out each writer's share.
2. Find out whether a publisher or administrator already represents you. If one does, ask whether they register your songs with the MLC, so you do not register twice.
3. If no one represents you, look at the MLC's registration process and register your works yourself.
4. Keep clean records: song title, writers, shares and the date of release.

The Copyright Office page does not set out step by step what a songwriter should do, and neither does this article replace the MLC's own instructions. Use the MLC site for the current registration details.

## Other places the mechanical right shows up

The blanket system is built for digital music providers. Releasing a cover is one common situation where an artist needs to think about licensing the song.

If you plan to release a cover, check the license requirements before the release date, not after. If you are leasing a beat or using a producer's work, read the agreement and find out who will own the composition and what you are allowed to do with it.

## Plan the release around the paperwork

Licensing is easiest to handle early. Before a release, confirm who wrote the song, who owns it and who is registered to be paid. That is much simpler than trying to fix it after the song is already live and earning.

## The takeaway

A mechanical license covers copying and distributing the composition of a song. The Music Modernization Act changed how digital use is licensed by creating a blanket system, and the Mechanical Licensing Collective administers it. If you write your own songs, register them, keep your records straight, and make sure you know who owns what before a release.
