You finish a song, record it, and release it. A listener presses play on a streaming service. It feels like one thing happening, but the music business treats it as several. This article follows a single release from the song you wrote to the stream a listener plays, and explains which parts belong to whom. It is general information and not legal advice.

## Start with two different things

A release holds two separate works. One is the song itself, meaning the music and lyrics. The other is the recording, meaning the specific performance captured and fixed in a track.

The U.S. Copyright Office makes this distinction in its guide to registering sound recordings. It says "the underlying music and lyrics are a 'musical work,'" and that a sound recording results from "the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds." It also says that "the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself" and "does not cover the music, lyrics, words, or other underlying content embodied in that recording."

In short, the song and the recording are not the same thing, even when one person made both.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office says the copyright in a sound recording does not cover the music or lyrics inside it. The song is a separate work.
:::

## Who is behind each one

The Copyright Office says the author of a sound recording is the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds. If the recording is made by employees as part of their jobs, the employer is the author as a work made for hire. If someone commissions it under a written work-made-for-hire agreement, that person is the author.

The same document does not discuss who owns the musical work. That is a different question. It can involve the songwriters and any publisher they work with. If you are releasing music, it is worth writing down who wrote the song and who owns the recording before you release it.

## Step one: the track is delivered

When you release music, a track goes out to streaming services. At that moment both works are in play. The service is using your recording, and the recording carries a song inside it.

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams lists the rights separately. For the composition it names the performance right and the mechanical reproduction right. For the sound recording it names the digital transmission performance right. It also lists sync and master use for placing music in film, television, commercials, and video games, though those uses come from someone licensing your work, not from a listener pressing play.

## Step two: the stream plays

On a streaming service where a listener picks the track they want, the service must be licensed to use the song. The Music Modernization Act (MMA) addresses this. The Copyright Office says the MMA "establishes a blanket licensing system for digital music providers." Its Title I covers permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams. Streaming services can use this single license instead of negotiating song by song.

The Copyright Office has designated The Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, "to collect and distribute mechanical royalty payments under Title I of the MMA." To receive payment under the blanket license, songwriters and publishers must register with The MLC. If you wrote the song, registering the work is a step that belongs to you or your publisher, not to the streaming service.

## Step three: the people who made the recording

The recording side has its own money path. The MMA also includes the Allocation for Music Producers Act, or AMP Act, as Title III. The Copyright Office says it "allows music producers, mixers, and sound engineers to receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings." It does this by setting up a process for SoundExchange, the designated collective, to distribute those royalties under a "letter of direction."

That means the people who helped make the recording can be paid through a defined process. If you work with a producer or engineer, it is worth knowing about the letter of direction before your release goes live.

## What to do before release day

A short checklist keeps the paperwork from catching up with you later.

Write down who wrote the song and what share each writer holds. Write down who owns the recording and who performed on it. Agree in writing how the producer and engineer will be credited and paid. Make sure the song is registered where the songwriter or publisher needs it to be, including with The MLC.

None of this has to be difficult. It is mostly a matter of recording decisions while everyone still remembers them.

## The short version

A release has a song and a recording, and they are separate works with separate rights. Streaming services license the song through the blanket license created by the Music Modernization Act, and The MLC collects and distributes those mechanical royalties. Producers, mixers, and engineers have their own route to royalties through SoundExchange under the AMP Act. Know who owns what before you release, and keep it in writing.
