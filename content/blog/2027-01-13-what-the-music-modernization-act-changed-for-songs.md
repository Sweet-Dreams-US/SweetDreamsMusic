Every song you hear on a streaming service is two pieces of property at once. One is the recording, the finished performance that was captured in a studio. The other is the song itself, the words and music as written. When an artist releases music, both pieces have to be licensed and paid for, and they follow different rules. The Music Modernization Act changed how the song side works for digital services. This post explains what it did in plain terms.

## Two things inside every release

A recording is the sound that was made. A musical work is the composition behind it: the melody, the lyrics, the structure. Different people can own each one. A songwriter may own the composition while a label or the artist owns the recording. A cover version has a new recording of an existing musical work, which is a clear example of why the two are separate.

The Music Modernization Act deals with both, but its best-known change is about the musical work and the way digital services get permission to use it.

## What the law is

The full name is the Orrin G. Hatch-Bob Goodlatte Music Modernization Act. The U.S. Copyright Office describes it as a major copyright law that updates the rules for the music marketplace. It has three parts, called titles.

Title I is the Music Licensing Modernization Act and covers downloads and streaming. Title II is the Classics Protection and Access Act, which brings pre-1972 sound recordings partly into the federal copyright system. Title III is the Allocation for Music Producers Act, which lets producers, mixers, and sound engineers receive royalties under the section 114 statutory license.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office lists the three titles as the Music Licensing Modernization Act, the Classics Protection and Access Act, and the Allocation for Music Producers Act.
:::

## The old way: one license per song

Before the change, a digital service that wanted to offer a song in a way that required a mechanical license had to get a section 115 license for each song. A mechanical license covers reproducing and distributing a musical work, which includes the copies made when a song is downloaded or streamed on demand.

Song-by-song licensing is a lot of paperwork when a service offers a very large catalog. It also depends on knowing who owns each song, and that information is not always easy to find.

## The blanket license

Title I replaces that song-by-song approach for musical works with a blanket license. According to the Copyright Office, digital music providers can use the blanket license to make and distribute digital phonorecord deliveries. That term covers permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams.

A blanket license covers many songs at once instead of one at a time. The Copyright Office says the new system took effect on the license availability date, January 1, 2021.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office states that the blanket license covers digital phonorecord deliveries, including permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams, and that the license availability date is January 1, 2021.
:::

## The Mechanical Licensing Collective

A blanket license needs someone to run it. The Copyright Office designated Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc., usually called the MLC, on July 8, 2019. It is a nonprofit.

According to the Copyright Office, the MLC receives notices and reports from digital music providers, collects and distributes royalties, and identifies musical works and their owners for payment. It also maintains a publicly accessible database of musical works, their shares, and, where known, their owners. Digital music providers fund the MLC's operations.

When the MLC cannot match a work to an owner, the royalties are treated as unclaimed. The Copyright Office says the MLC must publicize unclaimed royalties and provide a portal where songwriters can claim them, and that it would not distribute unclaimed funds before January 1, 2023.

There is also a second nonprofit, the Digital Licensee Coordinator. It coordinates the digital services that use the license and has a non-voting seat on the MLC board.

## What it means for songwriters and publishers

The MLC says it pays songwriters, composers, lyricists, and music publishers. Anyone can search its public works database without signing up. To claim works and receive payments, you join as a member and submit and maintain your song data through its portal. The MLC states that connecting with it is free.

This matters because a payment can only reach you if your song is matched to you. If a song's data is missing or wrong, the money can sit unmatched. Keeping accurate song information, such as the correct writers and shares, is part of getting paid.

## What it does not cover

The MLC handles mechanical royalties for musical works. It does not handle sound recording royalties, and it says it is not involved in public performance royalties. Digital services report usage of sound recordings, and the MLC matches that information to the musical works in its database.

That means a release still has several separate payment streams. The MLC is one of them, and it relates to the song, not the recording.

:::note Worth knowing
The MLC says it handles royalties for musical works, not sound recordings, and that it is not involved in public performance royalties.
:::

## Putting it to use

If you write or release music, a few habits follow from this. Know who wrote each song and what share each writer owns, and write it down before release. Check that your songs appear in the MLC database with correct information. Keep recordings and songs separate in your notes, because they are paid through different channels.
