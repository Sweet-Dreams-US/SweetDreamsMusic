You finish a track. The lyrics are done, the melody is set, and the final mix sounds right. Now you want to register it. The first question is what you are registering, because a finished track is really two works at once.

One is the song. The other is the recording of the song. They have different rules, different owners in some cases, and different places to register. This article explains the difference and how the two fit together when you plan a release. It is general information, not legal advice.

## Two works inside one track

The U.S. Copyright Office draws a clear line. A sound recording copyright covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics or words that are heard in the recording.

The musical work is the song: the music and the lyrics. The Copyright Office gives an example in Circular 56. The song "Rolling in the Deep" was written by Adele and Paul Epworth. That is the musical work. A recording of Aretha Franklin performing it is a separate thing, a sound recording of that performance.

Think of it this way when you describe your own release. The song is what you wrote. The recording is the performance that was captured and processed. If you wrote the song and also recorded it, you made both. If you recorded a song someone else wrote, you made only the recording.

## Who counts as the author of a recording

For a sound recording, the Copyright Office says the author is the performer and the producer who captured and processed the sounds. That can be one person or several. On a project where an artist performs and a separate producer records and mixes, both may be authors of the recording.

For the musical work, the authors are the people who wrote the music and the lyrics. These may be different people than the performers or the producer.

This is why it helps to settle who did what before you register. The application asks for author information, and it needs to match how the work was made. If you worked with other people, talk through credits and ownership first and put it in writing. A shared understanding at the start saves trouble later.

## Protection starts before you register

Registration is not what creates copyright protection for a sound recording. The Copyright Office states that neither registration nor publication is required for protection of sound recordings created after January 1, 1978, because those recordings are automatically protected.

Registering is still a separate step you can take. The sections below describe how the process works for recordings.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office notes that sound recordings created after January 1, 1978 are protected without registration. Registering is a separate step, not a requirement for the protection to exist.
:::

## How you register a recording

According to Circular 56, you file an online application and select "Sound Recording" on the Type of Work screen. Choosing the wrong type means starting over, so check this screen carefully.

You provide the title as it appears on the work, details about publication, and the author information. You then submit a deposit and a nonrefundable filing fee.

The deposit depends on how the recording is released. For works that are unpublished, or published only digitally, you upload a digital file of no more than 500 MB. For works published in physical form, such as CDs or LPs, you submit the physical format. Published U.S. works may also require two copies of the best edition, with a compact disc ranked first.

If you are releasing only online, the digital upload is the path that applies to you. If you press physical copies, plan for the physical deposit.

## How you register the song

Circular 56 covers sound recordings. For the musical work, it points readers to Circular 50 and to chapter 700 of the Compendium. Read those before you file for the song itself, since the steps are described there and not in the recording circular.

The general rule in Circular 56 is one application, one fee and one deposit per work. A song and its recording are two works, so by default they are two registrations.

The circular also points to Circular 56A, "Copyright Registration of Musical Compositions and Sound Recordings," for registering a sound recording together with the content embodied in it. If you wrote the song and made the recording yourself, that circular is the one to read to see whether a combined registration fits your situation.

## Registering with the MLC is another step

Copyright registration is not the only registration that matters for a song. Under the Music Modernization Act, the Copyright Office designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, to collect and distribute mechanical royalties. As of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive royalty payments under the blanket license for digital music providers.

That is a separate action from registering the copyright. Registering a song with the Copyright Office does not replace signing up with The MLC. If you write songs, plan for both.

:::note Worth knowing
The Music Modernization Act also has a title that lets music producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. The designated collective, SoundExchange, distributes those royalties through a process that uses a letter of direction.
:::

## A simple order of work for a release

Putting this together, here is a practical order. First, write down who wrote the song and who performed and produced the recording. Second, finish the final version, since the registration describes a specific work. Third, register the recording by choosing Sound Recording on the application. Fourth, register the song, using Circular 50 and the Compendium for the musical work. Fifth, if you are a songwriter, register with The MLC. Sixth, keep copies of your applications and confirmations with your release files.

Each step is short. The harder part is getting the credits right, so spend your time there.

## Keep your records together

When the paperwork is done, store it in one place. Keep the final audio file, the lyrics, the credits, the application confirmations and any agreements with collaborators. If a question comes up later about who made what, you can answer it quickly.

It also helps to note who contributed what on the day you record, since that decides who the authors are.
