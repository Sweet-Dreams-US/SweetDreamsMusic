Every finished song holds two different things. One is the song itself: the music and the lyrics. The other is the recording of someone performing it. The music business treats them as separate works with separate rights, and mixing them up causes real confusion when artists release music. This post explains the second one, the sound recording.

The definitions below come from the U.S. Copyright Office. This is general information for artists, not legal advice.

## Two works inside one song

The Copyright Office's Circular 56 draws the line clearly. The music and lyrics are what it calls a "musical work." A recording of someone performing that song is a "sound recording." The two are separate works.

A simple way to picture it: a songwriter can write a song and never record it. That song exists as a musical work. Ten different artists can later record that same song. Each of those recordings is its own sound recording.

## The definition

Circular 56 describes sound recordings as "works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds." The word "other" matters. A sound recording does not have to be music. A recorded spoken performance can be one too.

The copyright in a sound recording, according to the circular, "covers the recording itself." It protects the captured performance and production, not the content inside it.

The U.S. Copyright Office says the copyright in a sound recording "covers the recording itself," while the music and lyrics are a separate "musical work."
:::

## What "fixed" means

The definition depends on the word fixation. The circular says the sounds must be "captured in a medium from which they can be perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated." A sound recording is created when the sounds are first fixed in a phonorecord.

A phonorecord is the object that stores the sounds. The circular is careful to say that a sound recording is not the same thing as a phonorecord. The recording is the work. The phonorecord is the physical or digital thing that holds it, such as a file or a disc.

So a live performance that nobody captures is not a sound recording. The moment it is captured in a form that can be played back, a recording exists.

## Who the author is

The circular says the author of a sound recording is "the performer featured in the recording" and "the producer who captured and processed the sounds that appear in the final recording." Both can be authors, because the recording is a combination of the performance and the work of capturing and processing it.

The circular also notes that work-for-hire rules can change who the author is. If a recording is made under a work-for-hire arrangement, the authorship can sit with someone other than the performer or producer. That is a good reason to put agreements in writing before a session starts.

## What the copyright does not cover

The circular lists several limits. The copyright in a sound recording does not extend to the music, lyrics, words or other content embodied in the recording. Those belong to the musical work.

Sound recordings captured by purely mechanical means without originality of any kind lack enough authorship for protection.

And sounds that accompany a movie or other audiovisual work are not sound recordings. The circular treats them as part of that audiovisual work.

## Why video changes the picture

That last point matters to artists who release both audio and video. A music video is an audiovisual work, so the sounds in the video are treated as part of the video. A standalone audio release is a sound recording.

It is worth knowing the difference when you plan a release that includes a song and a video. The two outputs are different kinds of works under the definitions above.

## The Music Modernization Act and recordings

Congress changed some of the rules around recordings with the Orrin G. Hatch-Bob Goodlatte Music Modernization Act. The Copyright Office describes three parts.

Title III "allows music producers, mixers, or sound engineers to receive royalties" collected under the section 114 statutory license. SoundExchange distributes those royalties to them through a "letter of direction."

Title II "brings pre-1972 sound recordings partially into the federal copyright system."

Title I replaces the older track-by-track compulsory licensing system with a blanket license that covers downloads and streams. On July 8, 2019, the Copyright Office designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective as the coordinator for this system.

The detail to take from this: the people who make a recording, including producers, mixers and engineers, can be part of how recording royalties are paid.

## Separate rights, separate income

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams treats recordings and songs as separate rights with their own income paths. On the song side, it lists topics such as the performance right and the mechanical reproduction right of a composition. On the recording side, it lists the digital transmission performance right of a sound recording and the use of master recordings in television, film, commercials and video games.

The course description does not compare which pays more. It does say live music provides the bulk of revenue for musical artists, and that copyrights and other music royalty assets are growing in value.

For an artist, the lesson is practical. Knowing which part of a release is the recording and which part is the song tells you which rights are in play when you license, sell or share your work.

## A short summary

A song is a musical work. A recording of a performance of it is a sound recording. The recording is created when the sounds are fixed in a form that can be played back. Its copyright covers the recording itself, not the music and lyrics inside. The performer and the producer who captured the sounds are the authors, unless work-for-hire rules say otherwise.
