Artists often hear big numbers about streaming and small numbers about what they actually receive. Spotify publishes a public report called Loud & Clear that is meant to put real data behind that conversation. Reading it yourself is a good habit, because it shows you what streaming payouts look like at a platform level and what the report does not cover. This post explains what the report is, how to read it, and where it fits among the other ways an artist earns money.

## What Loud & Clear is

Loud & Clear is a website and annual report from Spotify. The site says it shares the data behind Spotify's royalty payments and explains how money moves through the streaming economy. The site shows data for 2025.

The report is written by the platform that makes the payments. That does not make it wrong, but it does mean you should read it as one source. It describes what Spotify pays and to whom. It does not describe what other services pay, and it does not describe the money an artist earns away from streaming.

:::note Worth knowing
Spotify's Loud & Clear site says it shares the data behind the company's royalty payments, and its Payouts section shows how many artists generate different levels of revenue each year.
:::

## Reading the report as an artist

Start with the parts that match your situation. The report is organized around topics such as total payouts, how many artists reach certain revenue levels, and how much comes from listeners in different countries. The revenue level figures matter most for a working independent artist, because they show how many artists reach each tier rather than only the biggest names.

When you read a number, ask three questions:

1. What is being counted? Total payouts to everyone is a different measure from what one artist receives.
2. Who receives it? Payments from a platform go to rights holders, such as labels, distributors and publishers. What reaches the artist depends on the agreements in place.
3. What period does it cover? A yearly figure tells you about that year only.

Writing down the answers keeps you from comparing a platform total with your own statement and drawing the wrong conclusion.

## Why a platform report is not your whole income

Streaming is one revenue stream. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams lists many more. It says live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists, and it names live performance as the primary source of income for performing artists. It also covers recording and streaming income, publishing and copyright income, synchronization in television, film, commercials and video games, merchandise, sponsorships and brand partnerships.

Seen this way, a streaming payout is a part of a larger picture. An artist might earn from a show, from a licensed song in a video, from a merchandise sale and from streams, all in the same month. A report about one platform cannot add those up for you.

:::

## Recording income and songwriting income

Two kinds of rights are involved when a song is streamed. One covers the recording, meaning the specific track that people hear. The other covers the composition, meaning the song itself as written. Different people can own each one, and they are often paid through different channels.

In the United States, the Music Modernization Act changed part of this. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that the law created a blanket licensing system for digital music providers to make and distribute things like downloads and interactive streams. It also explains that the Copyright Office designated The Mechanical Licensing Collective to collect and distribute the mechanical royalty payments under that system. Songwriters and music publishers need to register with the collective through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the blanket license.

The practical lesson is that writing and recording a song is only the first step. If you wrote the song, check that the composition is registered where the money for it is collected. A report from a streaming service cannot tell you whether you have done that.

## What to do with what you learn

Here is a short routine you can use each time a new edition of the report comes out:

1. Read the sections that match your stage as an artist.
2. Compare the report's tiers with your own streaming statements, keeping in mind that your statements come through your distributor or label.
3. List every other way you earned money in the last year.
4. Check that your songs are registered wherever songwriting royalties are collected.
5. Decide which one or two income streams you want to grow next.

This keeps the report in its place. It is a useful reference for understanding how streaming payments work, and it is one input to your plan, not the plan itself.

A public report can make a big average look like a normal result. It is not. Reports like this describe a platform, and your own numbers will depend on your music, your audience and your agreements.
