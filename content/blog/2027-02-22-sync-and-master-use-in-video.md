You have a song you love and a video you want to put it in. Maybe it is a music video, a behind the scenes reel, or a short clip for social media. The question that comes up is simple: do you need permission to use the song? The answer is often yes, and sometimes you need two permissions, not one. This article explains why.

## One song, two kinds of copyright

Every finished song has two different things inside it. The first is the song itself, meaning the music and the lyrics. The U.S. Copyright Office calls this the musical work. The second is a specific recording of that song, which the Copyright Office calls a sound recording.

Circular 56 from the Copyright Office describes a sound recording as a recorded performance, often of another work. It also says the underlying music and lyrics are a musical work, and a recording of someone performing that song is a sound recording.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office states that the copyright in a sound recording does not cover the music, lyrics, words, or other underlying content embodied in that recording.
:::

That sentence is the heart of the issue. Owning or controlling a recording does not mean you control the song inside it. The two can have different owners.

## Who is behind each one

Circular 56 explains that the authors of a sound recording are the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds. If they made it as employees, the employer is the author. If someone commissioned it under a written work made for hire agreement, that party is the author.

The song side is different. According to the Musicians Institute Library, a license for the composition is granted by the composer or their publisher. So a single track on a streaming service can point to one group for the recording and another group for the song.

## What a sync license covers

When you put a song in a video, you are synchronizing music with visual material. A synchronization license covers that use of the composition. The Musicians Institute Library describes it this way: the license covers the composition itself, not a recording. It adds that these licenses can usually be negotiated directly with the copyright owner or with their publisher.

So if you hire a band to write a new song for your video, and they own the song, a sync license from the writers or their publisher is the piece that covers the composition.

## What a master license covers

A master license covers the recording. The same library guide says a master license is created for people who wish to use a sound recording to create a new piece, and that it pays the owner of the specific recording you are using.

The guide gives an example. If you use a Bob Dylan recording of a Beatles song in a video, you would pay the owners of the composition under a sync license and pay Dylan under a master license. Two copyrights, two payments.

## Why you often need both

The library guide notes that when you get a synchronization license, it is most often paired with a master license. That is the practical answer to the question at the start of this article. If you are using an existing recording of an existing song, you usually need permission for the song and permission for that recording.

There is one situation where this gets simpler. If you wrote the song and you own the recording you made of it, you control both sides. That is common for artists releasing their own music, and it is one reason keeping clear records of who wrote and who owns what is worth the effort.

:::note Worth knowing
Berklee Online lists the synchronization of compositions and the use of master recordings in TV, film, commercials, and video games as one of the lessons in its music business revenue streams course. Sync and master use are treated as a source of income for the people who own the rights.
:::

## Questions to ask before you use a song in a video

A short list of questions keeps you out of trouble.

Who wrote the song, and who publishes it? That tells you who to ask about the composition.

Who owns this particular recording? That tells you who to ask about the master. If the recording is a cover, the answer may be different from the original.

Is it your own song and your own recording? If yes, you may already control both sides, but write it down, especially if other writers, performers, or a producer worked on it.

Where will the video be used? A permission for one use is not automatically a permission for another, so ask for the uses you plan, such as a music video, social clips, or both.

Is the agreement in writing? Verbal permission is hard to prove later.
