Artists often hear the word publishing and nod along without being sure what it means. It sounds like a business you hire later, after things are going well. In practice, publishing is already part of every song you write, and the choices you make about it early can decide whether royalties reach you or sit unclaimed.

This article explains it in plain terms, using educational material from the U.S. Copyright Office.

## Two copyrights in every song

Every recorded song contains two separate copyrights.

The first is the musical work. This is the composition and any lyrics. Composers and lyricists, the songwriters, hold these rights.

The second is the sound recording. This is the captured audio, created by performers, producers, or both. Record labels usually handle licensing for recordings.

The two can have different owners. A singer who performs a song they did not write owns part of the recording story but not the composition. A songwriter who writes for someone else holds the composition but not the recording. Publishing is the side of the business that deals with the musical work.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office describes the musical work as the composition and lyrics, and the sound recording as the captured audio. They are licensed and paid separately, which is why one song can generate several different kinds of royalty.
:::

## Performance rights

When a song is performed publicly, the musical work earns a performance royalty. Public performance includes radio, television, streaming, bars and arenas.

In the United States, performing rights organizations license these uses. The Copyright Office names ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR. They generally pay songwriters and publishers directly. Because these rights are non-exclusive, copyright owners can also license performances directly.

For a songwriter, this means joining a performing rights organization is how performance money normally finds you. Without a registered affiliation, there may be nobody collecting on your behalf.

## Mechanical rights

Mechanical rights cover reproducing and distributing a musical work in audio-only formats such as CDs, downloads and interactive streams.

Under the Section 115 compulsory license, other people can reproduce and distribute a musical work in those formats, and songwriters generally cannot refuse. In return, a royalty is owed.

For digital uses, the Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as the MLC, collects and distributes these royalties. The Copyright Office lists what you need to be paid. You must register with the MLC, have your songs matched to recordings, be identified as an owner, and provide payment and tax information.

For physical uses such as vinyl and CDs, you must appear in Copyright Office records. Record labels and agents such as the Harry Fox Agency or Music Reports often clear these uses.

## Print and sync

Publishers also commonly license sheet music, which is the print right, and synchronization uses, which put a song into film, television, ads and games.

There is an important difference here. Sync licenses have no compulsory option, so they are negotiated voluntarily. The mechanical license does not cover audiovisual works. If a song is going to appear in a video, someone needs to agree to that use. Permission for the audio alone is not the same thing.

## What a publisher does, and the ways to set it up

A publisher's job is to license, collect and distribute the money earned by the musical work. The Copyright Office describes several common arrangements.

In traditional publishing, the songwriter assigns copyright and receives the songwriter's share of royalties, while the publisher keeps the publisher's share.

In co-publishing, the songwriter keeps part of the copyright and part of the publisher's share.

In an administration deal, the administrator handles licensing, collection and distribution for a fee, typically a percentage of royalties, without taking ownership.

In sub-publishing, a foreign publisher exploits the works in another country.

In self-administration, the songwriter does these jobs directly.

Each arrangement is a trade between control and workload. Signing away copyright can bring help and reach. Keeping it means doing more yourself or paying someone to do it. A music attorney can explain how a specific offer would work.

## Recordings are a separate stream

For comparison, the money from the recording side works differently. The Copyright Office says the U.S. public performance right for sound recordings covers only digital audio transmissions, such as Spotify, Apple Music and Sirius XM. AM and FM radio and live venues do not pay for sound recordings.

Some digital services pay statutory royalties collected by SoundExchange. The Copyright Office gives this split: 50 percent to copyright owners, often labels, 45 percent to featured artists, 2.5 percent to non-featured musicians, and 2.5 percent to non-featured vocalists. Featured artists register with SoundExchange. Non-featured musicians and vocalists register with the AFM and SAG-AFTRA Intellectual Property Rights Distribution Fund.

:::note Worth knowing
Producers and engineers are paid by contract, or they can receive a share of featured artists' SoundExchange royalties through a letter of direction.
:::

## Registration and identifiers

Registration is where many artists lose track of their money.

For domestic works, copyright registration is required before filing a lawsuit. Registering before an infringement, or within three months of publication, also makes you eligible for statutory damages and attorneys' fees.

The industry also uses codes to identify things. An ISWC identifies a musical work. An IPI identifies writers and publishers. An ISRC identifies a recording. An ISNI can identify any type of creator. Having the right codes on your releases helps the right payments reach the right people.

A practical starting list for a new songwriter looks like this: know who wrote each song and in what shares, join a performing rights organization, register your works with the MLC, register your recordings and your featured artist status where it applies, and keep written agreements for every co-writer and collaborator.
