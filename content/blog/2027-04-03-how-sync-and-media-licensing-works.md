When a song plays in a TV show, a commercial, a film or a video game, someone gave permission for it to be there. That permission is called a sync license, short for synchronization license. For artists and songwriters it is one of the ways a song can earn money beyond streams and shows.

This post explains how sync works, why most placements need two licenses, and what to read carefully before you agree to one. It is general information, not legal advice. For a real deal, talk with a music lawyer.

## What sync means

Synchronization means pairing music with moving images. A song is "synced" when it is set to a scene, an ad or a game. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams lists the use of master recordings in TV, film, commercials and video games as its own revenue topic, alongside other ways copyrighted music earns money.

The key point is that this is a license, not a sale of the song. You are allowing a production to use it for specific purposes, and the terms of the license set the limits.

## The two licenses

A recorded song contains two separate copyrights. One covers the composition, which is the melody, harmony and lyrics. The other covers the sound recording, which is that particular recorded performance.

DISCO, a music licensing platform, explains that using existing music on screen requires two separate licenses, one for the song and one for the recording:

- The synchronization license comes from the publisher or songwriter who owns the composition.
- The master license, sometimes called the master use license, comes from whoever owns the recording, typically the record label or the recording artist.

An independent artist who wrote and owns the recording can often grant both. An artist with a label or a publisher may need each of them to agree. DISCO also notes that the two rights holders are under no obligation to match each other's terms or timeline.

:::note Why this matters
A production cannot use a song legally until both licenses are cleared. If only one side agrees, the placement cannot go ahead.
:::

## What a license spells out

Promise Legal, a law firm that writes about music contracts, lists the parts of a sync deal. These are the terms you should expect to see in writing:

1. Term: how long the production can use the song. This can be a fixed window, the run of a show, the life of the production or forever.
2. Territory: where the song can be used, such as worldwide or the United States only.
3. Media: which formats are covered, such as broadcast, theatrical, streaming, advertising or trailers. Promise Legal notes that any medium not listed is not licensed.
4. Exclusivity: whether you can license the same song to anyone else.
5. Use type: featured or background use, vocal or instrumental version, and whether it is used in context or out of context, as in a trailer or an ad.
6. Fee: usually a flat upfront payment, though it can also be an advance against royalties or a royalty-only deal.

A broader grant should cost more than a narrow one. A license for all media, worldwide and forever, is a very different deal from a one-year license for a regional ad.

## How you get paid

The sync fee is only part of the picture. Promise Legal points out that performance royalties are separate from the sync fee. They are paid when the production airs or streams. For a composition, the production's cue sheet tells the performing rights organizations, such as ASCAP and BMI, what music was used, so the song needs to be registered before it airs.

Mechanical royalties for digital services are a different matter again. The U.S. Copyright Office describes The MLC as the organization designated to collect and distribute mechanical royalties for digital music providers, and says songwriters and publishers must register with it to receive them. That system covers digital downloads and interactive streams, not placements in screen productions.

## What to check before you sign

Promise Legal's checklist for musicians is a good starting point:

- Get use, term, territory, media and exclusivity in writing.
- Reserve every right that is not granted.
- Ask for written credit, including the exact spelling of your name.
- Confirm who files the cue sheet, and put that duty in the contract.
- Confirm that co-writers, producers and labels have approved, so both licenses can close.
- Do not accept on the spot. Take time to review, and bring in a lawyer for large fees or broad grants.

## Where to start

If you have a song you think could fit a show, ad or game, begin by finding out who owns what. Check who wrote it, who owns the recording and whether any publisher or label holds rights. Register the song with a performing rights organization. Then, when an offer arrives, read the term, territory, media and exclusivity before you read the fee.
