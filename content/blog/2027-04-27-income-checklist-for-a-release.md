Most artists plan the release day. Fewer plan the money that follows it. A song can be streamed thousands of times and still leave royalties sitting unclaimed because nobody told the right organization that the song exists. This checklist walks through the accounts and registrations to sort out before a release goes live, so the income has somewhere to land.

It is a checklist, not legal or financial advice. Your own situation, your contracts and your collaborators decide the details. Use it to build a list of questions and tasks, then confirm each one with the organization or professional involved.

## Know that one song holds more than one right

A released song is not one asset. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams separates the rights in a composition from the rights in a sound recording. A composition has a performance right and a mechanical reproduction right, among others. A sound recording has its own rights, including a digital transmission performance right. Songs can also earn from synchronization, which is use in TV, film, commercials and video games.

Why this matters for a checklist: each right can be collected by a different organization. If you only set up one account, you may only be collecting one kind of income. Start by writing down who owns what on your release. Who wrote the song? Who owns the recording? Are there co-writers, producers or featured artists? You cannot register anything correctly until you know the answer.

Write the splits down before release day and have every collaborator agree to them. It is much easier to agree on shares when no money has arrived yet.

## Register the songwriting side for mechanical royalties

The U.S. Copyright Office explains that the Music Modernization Act set up a blanket licensing system so that digital music providers can distribute permanent downloads, limited downloads and interactive streams. The Mechanical Licensing Collective, called The MLC, is the entity the Copyright Office designated to collect and distribute the mechanical royalty payments under that system.

The Copyright Office page states that as of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC using its online claiming portal to receive royalty payments under the blanket license. The MLC itself says anyone owed digital audio mechanical royalties for their music in the U.S. should become a Member. It says streaming and download services send it usage data and royalties each month, it matches that usage to songs registered by Members, and it pays Members monthly.

The checklist items here are short:

- Decide whether you will register as a songwriter yourself or have a publisher or administrator do it.
- Register your songs in the MLC portal, with the correct writers and shares.
- Check that your song titles and writer names are spelled the same way everywhere.

## Set up the recording side

The sound recording earns on a separate track. Berklee Online lists the digital transmission performance right of a sound recording as its own revenue stream, along with interactive streaming revenue and non-interactive webcasting and satellite radio. Title III of the Music Modernization Act also lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties for uses of sound recordings. The Copyright Office says it codifies a process for SoundExchange, the designated collective, to distribute those royalties under a letter of direction.

Practically, that means you should ask three questions before release. Who is collecting for the recording? Is every featured artist and every producer who should be paid listed? If a producer or engineer is owed a share, has a letter of direction been discussed? If you do not know, ask the distributor or the organization directly. Do not guess.

## Check how your streaming income is reported

Spotify runs a site called Loud & Clear that says its goal is to give artists transparency about how music streaming works, and that it shares data behind Spotify's royalty payments and explains how money flows through the global streaming economy. The site links to Spotify's royalties guide for artists at artists.spotify.com/royalties-guide.

You do not need to master the whole system before you release. You do need to know where your streaming statements will arrive, who receives them, and how often. Create the artist account, confirm that the name on the account matches the name on your registrations, and read the royalties guide once. Keep a simple note of which service pays which right.

## Look past streaming

Berklee Online's course covers more than streams. It lists live performer fees, sponsorships and brand partnerships, artist-branded merchandise, crowdfunding, grants, residencies, and prizes, and teaching as other income sources. Not every artist will use all of them, and a release does not have to earn from every one.

For a release checklist, pick the few that fit your situation. If you play live, plan how the release supports booking. If you sell merchandise, plan what ships with the single. If you are applying for a grant, keep your release documents in order. The point is to decide on purpose instead of finding out later what you missed.

## Keep one record of everything

Once the registrations and accounts exist, put them in one place. A single document with the following is enough:

- Each song title, writer names and agreed shares.
- Who owns the recording and who is owed a share of it.
- Which organization collects each right, with login details stored safely.
- The date you registered each song and any confirmation you received.
- The statements you expect and when.

Update it for every release. When a collaborator asks months later why a payment looks different, the record answers the question.

## The short version

Before a release, confirm the splits, register the songs for mechanical royalties, set up the recording side, create your streaming accounts, pick the other income sources that fit you, and write it all down. None of it is exciting. All of it decides whether the money you earn can find you.
