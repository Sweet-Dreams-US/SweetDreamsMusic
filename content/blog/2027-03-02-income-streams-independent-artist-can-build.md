Most independent artists do not earn from one place. Money comes from several smaller sources, and each one works differently. Knowing the list helps you decide where to spend your time and what to set up before you release a song. This article walks through the main income streams for an artist working without a label, using the course outline from Berklee Online as the guide.

## Why one stream is rarely enough

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams says that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. The same description says artists need to cultivate ancillary income sources just to survive. In plain terms, an artist who depends on one source is exposed when that source slows down.

The outline does not give percentages or payout rates for any stream, so treat this article as a map, not a forecast. What follows is a list of places money can come from, not a promise of how much.

:::

## Live performance

Live shows involve promoters, venues, festivals and talent agents. The Berklee outline also covers what it calls getting to the net, which means the artist's pay after deductions are taken out. The number a venue quotes is not always the number an artist keeps, so it helps to ask what comes out of a payment before agreeing to a date.

The outline also lists the people who often work around a touring artist: managers, business managers and attorneys. An independent artist may hold several of those jobs alone at the start.

## Publishing and composition rights

When you write a song, you own a composition, and the composition earns money separately from the recording. Berklee lists several kinds of publishing rights: performance, mechanical, derivative and print rights, plus grand or dramatic rights.

One practical point comes from the U.S. Copyright Office. Under the Music Modernization Act, songwriters and music publishers have needed to register with The Mechanical Licensing Collective, called The MLC, through its online claiming portal since January 1, 2021 to receive royalty payments under the new blanket license. If you write your own songs, that registration is a task for your release checklist.

:::note Registering with The MLC
The U.S. Copyright Office says that as of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive payments under the blanket license.
:::

## Recorded music and streaming

The recording of a song earns separately from its composition. The Berklee outline names the kinds of digital use of sound recordings: interactive on-demand streaming, non-interactive webcasting and satellite radio. Each has its own payment path.

How your recordings reach those services, and who collects on your behalf, depends on the companies you choose. The outline does not describe those steps, so read the terms of any service before you sign up, and keep a copy of what you agreed to.

## Sync and media licensing

Sync means placing a song or a recording in other media. Berklee's list covers TV, film, commercials and video games. Both the composition and the master recording can be licensed, and they may have different owners. If you own both, you can license both.

The outline does not give payment figures for sync, so do not count on it as a steady source. Treat it as one stream among several, and keep your song files, credits and ownership details organized so you can answer quickly if someone asks to license a track.

## Merchandise, sponsorships and brand partnerships

The outline also covers branding and merchandise: sponsorships, endorsements, brand partnerships, merchandise and artist-branded instruments. This is the stream where your audience buys something tied to you directly, so it depends on having a recognizable look and a way to reach fans.

## Other sources

Berklee also lists income that does not fit the groups above: featured artist and producer income, advances and royalties, union pay and benefits, crowdfunding, grants, residencies, prizes and teaching. It even includes treating copyrights as financial assets.

You do not need all of these. The point is that a working artist can mix several, and that many of them are open to someone without a label.

## How to use this list

Start by writing the streams down and marking which ones you already have, which ones you could start this year, and which ones do not fit your music. Then pick one new stream to set up before your next release. For many artists that is the songwriter registration, because it is free to do early and hard to fix late.

Keep a simple record of where each payment comes from. When income arrives from five places in five formats, a clear log is what lets you see which ones are working.
