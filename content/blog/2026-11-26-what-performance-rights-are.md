When a song is played where the public can hear it, the songwriter and the publisher can be owed money. The right behind that payment is called the public performance right. This article explains what it is, who collects the money and how the system works. It is written for artists and songwriters who are learning how the music business works. It is general information and not legal advice.

## What the public performance right is

U.S. copyright law gives the owner of a musical work a list of exclusive rights. Section 106 of the Copyright Act lists them, and one of them is the right to perform the work publicly. In plain terms, the owner of a song gets to decide who may play it for the public and can expect to be paid for it.

The Congressional Research Service describes the right this way: it prohibits others from streaming, broadcasting or playing a musical work for public listening without permission.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Act says a work is performed publicly when it is performed at a place open to the public, or at a place where a substantial number of people outside a normal circle of family and social acquaintances are gathered. It also covers transmitting a performance to the public.
:::

## The song and the recording are different things

One recording carries two separate pieces of copyright. The musical work is the song itself, meaning the melody and the lyrics. The sound recording is the specific recorded performance of that song.

The law treats their performance rights differently. For musical works, the right covers performing the work publicly. For sound recordings, Section 106 gives a narrower right: the right to perform the recording publicly by means of a digital audio transmission. The performing rights organizations described below deal with the public performance of musical works, which means the song.

## Why performing rights organizations exist

A songwriter cannot follow every song across every venue, station and service. The Congressional Research Service explains that tracking each copyright holder and each performance was difficult, so songwriters and publishers formed performing rights organizations, known as PROs, and assigned them their public performance rights.

A PRO licenses the songs in its catalog to the places that play them, collects the money and pays it out. The report says that after charging administrative fees, PROs split the public performance royalties they collect among their members, generally based on how often their works are played.

## The four PROs in the United States

The Congressional Research Service names four. ASCAP was founded by songwriters in 1914 and is a nonprofit. BMI was founded in 1939 by broadcasters and is also a nonprofit. SESAC was founded in 1930. It is a for-profit corporation with invitation-only membership. GMR was founded in 2013 and is also a for-profit corporation.

The report also says ASCAP and BMI have long operated under consent decrees with the U.S. Department of Justice. Those decrees require them to license to applicants on equal terms and to admit qualifying songwriters. The report was published in March 2020, and the Department of Justice was reviewing those decrees at the time, so a reader who needs the current rules should check them again.

:::note Worth knowing
Per the Congressional Research Service, a business that wants to play music publicly generally obtains a blanket license, which lets it perform any of the songs in a PRO's catalog. The fee is either a flat rate or a percentage of total revenue.
:::

## What a blanket license means for you

A venue, a station or a streaming service does not negotiate with each songwriter. It buys a blanket license from a PRO. That license lets it play any song in the PRO's catalog. The PRO then passes the royalties to the writers and publishers it represents.

For a songwriter, this means performance royalties are not negotiated one venue at a time. The Congressional Research Service says songwriters and publishers assign their public performance rights to PROs, so the royalties for those performances come through the PRO. Each PRO has its own membership terms, and the report notes that SESAC is invitation-only, so check the terms of any PRO before you decide which one to approach.

The fee a business pays also varies. The Congressional Research Service says it is either a flat rate or a percentage of the business's total revenue, which is why two venues can pay different amounts for the same blanket license.

## How this connects to the rest of your release

Performance royalties are one stream of money in the music business, next to others such as mechanical licenses, which cover reproducing and distributing a song. The Music Modernization Act, which the Copyright Office describes on its site, changed how mechanical licensing works for musical works. The Copyright Office says its first title replaced the song-by-song compulsory licensing structure for musical works with a blanket licensing system. Keeping the streams straight helps you know what you are owed, and from whom, before you release.
