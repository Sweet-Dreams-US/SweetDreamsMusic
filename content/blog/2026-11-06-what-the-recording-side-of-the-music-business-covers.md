When people say they work in music, they usually mean one of a few different businesses. One of them is recorded music: the business of making sound recordings, owning them and getting them heard. If you are an artist about to put out a single, this is the part of the industry you will deal with first. Knowing what it covers helps you ask better questions before you spend money or sign anything.

This article explains the recording side in plain terms. It covers what a sound recording is, who counts as its author, what it means to release one, how registration works, and which recent laws touch recordings.

## What a sound recording is

A sound recording is a captured performance. The U.S. Copyright Office describes sound recordings as "works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds." In everyday terms, the moment a performance is saved to a file, tape or disc, there is a recording.

The recording is its own thing. The Copyright Office says its copyright covers the recording itself, not the song that happens to be on it. A song and a particular recording of that song are two distinct works. We will cover that split in a separate article, but keep it in mind here, because everything on the recording side is about the recording and not the composition.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Copyright Office treats a song and a specific recording of it as two distinct works. The copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself.
:::

## Who the author of a recording is

Many artists assume the person whose name is on the release is the only author. The Copyright Office describes it differently. For a sound recording, it says the authors are the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds.

That has a practical meaning. If you hire a producer to record and mix your track, the producer may be an author of that recording alongside you. Who owns what can then depend on agreements made before or during the session. The Copyright Office also explains two cases where someone else is treated as the author. If a performer or producer made the recording as an employee in a typical employment relationship, the employer is the author as a work made for hire. If a third party commissioned the recording as part of a collective work and the parties agreed in writing that it is a work made for hire, the third party is the author.

The lesson is simple. Decide who owns the recording before the session, and put it in writing.

## What making a recording involves

The recording side starts with the session itself: capturing the performance, then shaping the sound through editing and mixing. The Copyright Office's wording about the producer, who "captured and processed the sounds," reflects this. Both steps count as authorship, which is why the producer is named alongside the performer.

For an artist, the practical questions at this stage are who is paying for the session, who is making creative decisions, and who will hold the final files. Those answers feed directly into ownership, so they are worth settling early.

## What releasing a recording means

Releasing a recording sounds like a single event, but the law has a specific meaning for publication. The Copyright Office says publication means distributing copies of a recording to the public by sale, transfer, rental, lease or lending, or offering them to a group for further distribution or public performance.

It also makes a point that surprises many people: simply performing a sound recording publicly does not count as publication. Playing a track at a show or on a stream does not by itself make it published in the legal sense.

This matters for registration, because the Office handles unpublished works, digitally published works and physically published works a little differently.

## How registration works

Registering a sound recording means submitting an application, paying the filing fee and providing a deposit of the work. Per the Copyright Office, you select "Sound Recording" as the type of work at the start. Choosing the wrong type means starting over, so check it before you submit.

For unpublished works, or works published only digitally, the Office says to upload through its online system and not send a physical copy as well. Each file must be in an acceptable format and under 500 MB. Works published in physical form follow different deposit rules.

If you have several unpublished recordings, there is a shortcut worth knowing. The Office describes a group registration option that covers up to ten unpublished recordings by the same authors on one application.

Registration is a decision for each artist and release. If you have questions about your own rights, a music attorney can answer them. Nothing here is legal advice.

## Recent laws that touch recordings

Three parts of the Music Modernization Act matter on the recording side. The Copyright Office describes them this way.

Title I replaces song-by-song licensing for digital music providers with a blanket license, covering digital downloads and interactive streams. It also set up a Mechanical Licensing Collective to administer it. That part mainly concerns songs, but it shapes how digital music is licensed.

Title II, the Classics Protection and Access Act, brings recordings made before February 15, 1972 partly into the federal copyright system and gives their owners infringement remedies.

Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license. SoundExchange distributes those royalties through a letter of direction.

:::note Worth knowing
Under Title III of the Music Modernization Act, music producers, mixers and sound engineers can receive royalties collected under the section 114 license, distributed by SoundExchange through a letter of direction.
:::

## Putting it together before you release

Recorded music covers three jobs: making the recording, owning it, and releasing it. A short checklist helps. Know who the authors are and write down who owns the recording. Keep the final files organized. Understand whether your release counts as published. Decide whether to register the work.
