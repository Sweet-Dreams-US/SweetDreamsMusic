Most musicians do not live off one paycheck. They earn from several places at once, and each place works by its own rules. If you are new to releasing music, it helps to see the whole list before you decide where to spend your time and money first.

This article walks through the main income streams, in plain terms. It is a map, not a plan. Which streams matter most for you depends on the kind of music you make, how you perform, and what you own.

## Live performance

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams calls live music the "primary breadwinner for performing artists." Shows, festival slots and private bookings are paid in the moment, and the pay is tied to you being there.

Live income also feeds the other streams. A good show sells merchandise, brings new listeners to your recordings, and gives you footage to share afterward. That is why live work and online work tend to rise together instead of competing.

## Branding and merchandise

The same course groups sponsorships, endorsements, brand partnerships and merchandise under branding. Merchandise is the part most new artists can start with: shirts, posters, and anything else your audience would wear or keep.

Brand deals come later, once you have an audience a company wants to reach. The course also lists legacy and expanded brands, such as artist-branded merchandise, tribute bands and artist-branded instruments. Those are long-term examples, but they show how a name can earn beyond the music itself.

## Composition copyrights

A song is a copyrighted work, separate from any recording of it. The Berklee course lists several rights that can earn income from a composition: the performance right, the mechanical reproduction right, the derivative right, the print right and the grand right. It also lists synchronization licensing, which is the use of a song in TV, film, commercials and video games.

If you write your own songs, you are the starting point for all of these. Keeping track of what you wrote, who co-wrote it, and who owns which share is the first step to being paid for any of them.

:::note Worth knowing
Under the Music Modernization Act, songwriters and music publishers must register with The Mechanical Licensing Collective (The MLC) through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the new blanket license for digital music providers. This applies as of January 1, 2021, according to the U.S. Copyright Office.
:::

## Sound recording copyrights

A recording is its own copyrighted work. Berklee lists two ways it earns: the digital transmission performance right, and licensing of master recordings. If you own your masters, you decide who may use them and on what terms. If someone else owns them, they collect that income and you may be paid through a royalty instead.

Recording income also includes advances and royalties for featured artists and producers. These depend on the contract you signed, so read any agreement before you sign it.

## Streaming and broadcast

The course splits this stream in two. Interactive streaming is on-demand listening, where the listener picks the song. Non-interactive webcasting and satellite radio are the other kind, where the listener does not choose the exact track.

Each kind is paid through its own channel, which is why one song can generate several small payments from different places. The Music Modernization Act also changed who can be paid. Its Title III lets music producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings, and it sets a process for SoundExchange to distribute those royalties under a letter of direction.

## Union income

Members of the AFM and SAG-AFTRA can earn pay and benefits through those unions, including health and pension benefits, according to the Berklee course. Not every artist will join a union, but session players and performers who work on union projects should know the option exists.

## Alternative funding and teaching

Not all income comes from selling music or tickets. The Berklee course lists crowdfunding, grants, residencies and prizes under alternative funding. It also lists teaching: many musicians earn from passing on what they know to the next group of players.

These streams are steady in a way that a single release is not. A weekly lesson schedule, for example, pays on a calendar, not on a chart position.

## Putting the streams together

Few artists use every stream, and few rely on one. A reasonable way to start is to ask three questions.

First, what do you already own? Your songs and your recordings are the base, so register and protect them. Second, where do your listeners already meet you? If it is live shows, build around that. If it is video and short clips, build around that. Third, what can you repeat on a schedule? Teaching, a monthly release, or a regular show all give you income you can plan around.
