A release looks simple from the outside. There is a song, maybe a video, and a date. Behind it, several people make decisions, and each one has a different job. Knowing who does what tells you who to call when a problem comes up, and what each person usually costs.

The figures and role descriptions below come mainly from Emily White, a long-time artist manager, in an article adapted from her book on building a sustainable music career. Her numbers are typical ranges, not rules. Every deal is negotiated, so treat them as a starting point for your own questions.

## The artist sets the direction

The artist makes the music and decides what kind of career to build. Everyone else on this list works to support those decisions. That sounds obvious, but it matters when you are handed a contract or a plan. If you do not understand what a person will do for you and what they will be paid, you are not ready to sign with them.

Berklee Online describes live music as the bulk of revenue for musical artists and says artists need to build other income sources to get by. That is why the team around an artist usually covers more than the release itself. Shows, merchandise, licensing and releases all feed one career.

## The manager runs the plan

According to White, a manager sets short-term and long-term goals, puts together the team and directs it. Managers increasingly handle distribution and promotion too, and often act the way a label would.

Pay is usually a 15 percent commission on gross earnings across all parts of the career. Some deductions, such as merchandise costs, can be negotiated. Some managers take half the commission on gross merchandise sales. Early in a career, some managers work for a salary instead.

:::note Worth knowing
White writes that managers are traditionally entitled to diminishing commissions after an artist and manager split. Ask how long that tail lasts before you agree to a management deal.
:::

## The agent books the shows

An agent handles live bookings and negotiates the terms of shows. White notes that an agent can often take on more artists than a manager can, because the work is narrower.

The usual pay is 10 percent of gross earnings from live shows. An agent is not paid on your streaming income, merchandise or other parts of your career under that arrangement. The commission follows the work.

## The attorney reads the contracts

A music attorney reviews significant agreements, such as a contract with a producer, a label, a publisher or a manager. The review happens before you sign, which is the only time it can change anything.

White gives hourly rates of $300 to $750, often with an upfront retainer of about $1,500 to $2,500. Some attorneys work for a 5 percent commission on earnings, which is lower than a manager's rate. Berklee Online lists the attorney, the personal manager, the business manager and the talent agent as separate topics in its music business revenue course, which shows how standard this division of work is.

## The business manager handles the money

A business manager is a bookkeeper or accountant who specializes in music and entertainment. White lists budgets, insurance, paying musicians and crew, and taxes among the tasks, with tax work done by those who are CPAs. A business manager can also help set up business entities.

Pay is hourly, or a 5 percent gross commission on all earnings. Money questions get harder as income grows and comes from more places, so this role tends to matter more after the first few releases than before.

## What to do when you have none of these people

Many independent artists release music with no manager, agent, attorney or business manager. The jobs still exist. Someone has to plan the rollout, someone has to book the first shows, someone has to read the distribution terms, and someone has to track the money. When you have no team, you are doing those jobs yourself.

A practical way to cover them is to write the tasks down and mark each one as something you can do, something you can learn, or something to hire for. Hire for the tasks where a mistake is expensive and hard to undo. A contract is one. A content calendar is not.

## Questions to ask before you bring anyone on

Before you agree to work with any of these roles, get clear answers to a short list of questions:

- What percentage do you take, and on which income?
- Is the commission on gross or on net earnings?
- How long does the agreement last, and how does it end?
- What do you earn after we stop working together, and for how long?
- Which tasks are yours, and which stay with me?

Ask for the answers in writing. If a person is vague about how they are paid, that tells you something. Spoken promises are hard to prove later, and a short written summary protects both sides.

In short, the artist sets the direction. The manager runs the plan, usually for about 15 percent. The agent books shows, usually for about 10 percent of live earnings. The attorney reads the contracts, usually by the hour. The business manager keeps the books and handles taxes, by the hour or for about 5 percent. You may need all of them eventually, and you may need none of them for your first release. What you cannot skip is knowing which jobs need doing and who is doing each one.
