When a song plays in a TV episode, a film scene, a commercial or a video game, someone had to get permission first. That permission is called a synchronization license, usually shortened to a sync license. For an artist, sync is one of several ways a song can earn money, and it works differently from streaming. This article explains what sync licensing is, which licenses are involved, who grants them, and how the fees get set.

## What "sync" means

The word comes from synchronization, which means pairing music with moving images. The Mechanical Licensing Collective (The MLC) describes a synchronization license as permission to incorporate a musical work, a sound recording, or both, in visual media. That covers TV shows, films, ads, online video and video games.

The license comes from the copyright owner or the person who administers the copyright for them. The company that wants to use the song has to contact that owner and negotiate the terms. Nobody hands out a standard license at a fixed price.

## Two rights, two licenses

A recorded song contains two separate pieces of copyright. One is the composition, which is the underlying song and its lyrics. The other is the master, which is the specific recorded performance of that song.

Using a recorded song in a video usually means clearing both:

- The sync license covers the composition. It is granted by the publisher or the songwriter.
- The master use license covers the recording. It is granted by the record label or the recording artist.

Soundcharts notes that a music supervisor has to clear each of these separately, even when the same person owns both. An artist who writes and records their own music and has no label or publisher may be the one granting both licenses. That is a good reason to know which rights you own.

:::note Worth knowing
A cover recording can lower costs for the person licensing the music. Because the master belongs to whoever made the cover, they only need a sync license from the owner of the underlying composition.
:::

## How sync pays differently from streaming

Sync is a negotiated fee for placing a song in a piece of content. It is paid by the company using the music, and it is usually a one-time payment for the agreed use.

The MLC states plainly that it does not administer synchronization licenses or distribute synchronization royalties. The MLC's job is mechanical royalties for digital music services, as set up by the Music Modernization Act. So registering a song for streaming mechanicals does not license or collect anything for sync. Sync deals are handled directly between the parties.

A placement can also lead to other income. Soundcharts points out that when a song is performed publicly, performance royalties collected through performing rights organizations such as ASCAP or BMI can apply as well.

## How the fee gets set

There is no standard rate. The MLC says fees range from small amounts for lesser-known works to hundreds of thousands of dollars for a popular song.

According to Soundcharts, the artist side weighs the money offered against the promotional value of the placement. A developing artist in a major film may accept less for the exposure. An established artist whose song will sell a major brand's product may ask for much more.

The scope of use also changes the price. Soundcharts lists the license term, the territory, and the nature of the use, such as background music, a featured scene, a theme or end credits. Broader and more central use gives the artist more room to negotiate.

:::note Worth knowing
Soundcharts reports that licensing representatives who pitch songs for sync typically keep 20 to 65 percent of the sync value. Ask any rep what share they keep before you sign.
:::

## Where an independent artist starts

Most of the work before a first placement is preparation. A few practical points follow from what the sources describe:

- Know who owns the composition and who owns the master. If you wrote and recorded the song yourself, confirm that nobody else has a claim on either one.
- Keep your information organized. Someone clearing a song needs to find the owner quickly, so make your contact details easy to reach.
- Be ready to discuss scope. Expect to be asked about term, territory and type of use, because those shape the fee.
- Read agreements before agreeing to them. Representation deals take a share of the fee, so know the percentage before you sign.

This is general information and not legal advice. For a specific agreement, a music attorney can review the terms.

## Why the details matter

Because sync is negotiated one deal at a time, small details in the agreement carry real weight. The length of the license, the countries it covers and the type of media all affect what the song can be used for and what you are paid. A license for one TV episode in one territory is a different deal from a license for a worldwide ad campaign. Before agreeing, make sure you understand which uses are allowed, how long the permission lasts, and whether the agreement is exclusive or leaves you free to license the same song elsewhere. If anything is unclear, ask for it in writing.

## The short version

Sync licensing is permission to pair a song with moving images. It usually involves two licenses, one for the composition and one for the master. Both are negotiated directly with the owners, the fee depends on the use and the artist, and it is separate from the mechanical royalties the MLC handles for streaming. Knowing what you own is the first step to taking part in it.
