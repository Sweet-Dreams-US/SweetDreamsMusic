If you are an artist who is trying to work out how music can pay your bills, you will hear one answer again and again: no single source does it alone. Streaming, shows, publishing, licensing, merchandise and other work all pay in different ways and at different times. Most working artists put several of them together. This post walks through the main sources, how each one pays, and why the combination matters.

## Each source pays differently

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams covers a long list of ways artists earn. It groups them into live performance, streaming and recorded music, publishing and copyright, branding and merchandise, and a set of other sources such as union work, crowdfunding, grants, residencies, prizes and teaching.

These sources do not behave alike. Some pay per play, some pay per show, some pay once when a license is signed, and some pay only when a fan buys something from you directly. An artist who relies on one of them has one way to be paid. An artist with several has more than one way, and the sources can cover for each other when one is slow.

:::note Where the money has traditionally come from
Berklee Online's course description says that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. The course page is an outline and does not give payment amounts for any stream.
:::

## Recorded music and streaming

Recorded music is the part most new artists think of first. Berklee's course covers interactive, on-demand streaming revenue, along with non-interactive webcasting and satellite radio, and it covers featured-artist advances and royalties.

Streaming totals are large across the industry. Spotify's Loud & Clear report says Spotify paid the music industry over $11 billion in 2025. The same report says more than 13,800 artists earned at least $100,000 from Spotify alone that year, and that the artist ranked 100,000th earned more than $7,300, compared with about $350 in 2015.

Those numbers show two things at once. Streaming can support a career, and Spotify says its payouts rose more than 10 percent year over year. They also show that the amount reaching a typical artist from one platform is far smaller than the headline total. That gap is the first reason artists add other sources.

## Publishing and songwriting income

If you write songs, there is a second layer of income on top of the recording. Berklee's outline lists the performance right in compositions, the digital transmission performance right in sound recordings, and mechanical reproduction, along with sync licensing for television, film, commercials and video games.

The U.S. Copyright Office explains one part of how this works in the United States. The Music Modernization Act created a blanket license that lets digital music providers make and distribute downloads and interactive streams. The Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, collects and distributes the mechanical royalties under that license. Since January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers have had to register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the blanket license.

The practical lesson is that songwriting income does not always arrive on its own. If you wrote a song, check that it is registered where it needs to be.

## Producers and engineers can be paid too

The same Act has a section for the people who make the recordings. The Copyright Office says Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets music producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. It sets up a process for SoundExchange, the designated collective, to pay these royalties under a letter of direction.

If you work with a producer or engineer, this is worth asking about early, so the right paperwork is in place.

## Live shows, merchandise and brand work

Berklee's outline covers live performance in depth, including the roles of promoters, venue owners, talent agents and festivals. It also covers sponsorships, endorsements and brand partnerships, and merchandise beyond the tour.

These sources have a feature that streaming does not. They are tied to people showing up, buying a shirt or hiring you. That makes them well matched to content, because every video, photo and post gives a fan a reason to see you play or buy something from you.

## Other work that fits around music

Berklee also lists union work through AFM and SAG-AFTRA, which can include health and pension benefits, along with crowdfunding, grants, residencies, prizes and teaching. For many artists these fill the gaps between releases and tours.

## Reach outside your home market

Spotify's report adds one more point for planning. It says artists typically earn more than half of their royalties from outside their home country, even within two years of their debut. It also says about 85 percent of the artists who passed $100,000 in 2025 are based outside the United States.

An artist who thinks only about a local audience may be missing part of where streaming income comes from.

## How to put the sources into a plan

A simple way to start is to write down every source from the list above and mark which ones you can act on this year. Then add a rough date for each. Streaming starts when you release. Publishing registration should happen before or at release. A video or photo set can be made around the release date. A show or a merchandise drop gives fans a place to act on the attention the release creates.

You do not need all of them at once. You do need to know which ones you are building, because each of them takes planning, and the ones you plan are the ones that pay.
