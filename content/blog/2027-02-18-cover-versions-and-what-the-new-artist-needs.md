Recording a cover is one of the most common ways a new artist gets heard. People already know the song, so they click. But a cover is not free to release just because you performed it yourself. It touches two separate pieces of copyright, and a new artist needs to understand both before pressing release.

This post explains the difference, what a cover artist can and cannot do, and the practical steps to take first. It is general information, not legal advice. For a specific release, check with the rights holders, your distributor, or a music attorney.

## A song and a recording are two different things

The first idea to get straight is that a song and a recording of that song are separate works. The U.S. Copyright Office makes this point in Circular 56. It describes a sound recording as the recorded performance and the musical work as the underlying song, the music and lyrics. Its own example treats a famous song and a famous singer's recording of it as two distinct works.

That matters for covers. When you record someone else's song, you are creating a new sound recording of an existing musical work. The recording can be yours. The song is not.

:::note Worth knowing
Circular 56 says the copyright in a sound recording does not cover the music, lyrics, or other underlying content embodied in the recording.
:::

## What you own and what you do not

When you make a cover, the new recording is the part you and your team created. The Copyright Office says the author of a sound recording is the featured performer and the producer who captured and processed the sounds, with some exceptions for employment and work made for hire agreed in writing. That is one reason it helps to have clear written agreements with a producer, engineer, or studio before you start.

The song itself still belongs to the writer or publisher. Registering your recording does not give you rights in the song. Circular 56 states this directly, and it points readers to a different circular for registering songs.

## The mechanical license for covers

So how does an artist legally release a recording of someone else's song? One route in the United States is the compulsory license for making and distributing phonorecords, which the Copyright Office describes in Circular 73.

The license becomes available once phonorecords of a nondramatic musical work have been distributed to the public in the United States with the copyright owner's authority. It comes with limits. The primary purpose has to be distributing recordings to the public for private use, which includes digital delivery. You may adjust the arrangement only as needed to fit the style of your performance. You cannot change the basic melody or the fundamental character of the song.

You are also not required to use it. You can negotiate directly with the owner. The compulsory license is there for cases where the owner will not negotiate or cannot be reached.

:::note Worth knowing
Circular 73 explains that the compulsory license is obtained by serving a Notice of Intention, and that royalties are paid monthly once the owner is identified.
:::

## Digital releases and the MLC

Most new artists release digitally, so the Music Modernization Act is relevant. The Copyright Office explains that Title I of the Act set up a blanket licensing system for digital music providers to make and distribute digital phonorecord deliveries, such as downloads and interactive streams. It also designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective, called The MLC, to collect and distribute mechanical royalties under that license.

For a cover artist, the practical point is that the rules for digital distribution have changed and are not the same as the older process. Ask your distributor how it handles covers, and what information it needs from you about the original song, before you upload.

## What a license does not cover

A mechanical license is narrow. Circular 73 says the license does not cover sound recordings or phonorecords used for background music, jukeboxes, broadcasting, or other public uses, which need permission from the copyright owner. It also describes sounds that accompany a motion picture or other audiovisual work as outside its definition of a phonorecord.

In plain terms, a license to release an audio cover does not by itself give you permission to put that recording under a video. A music video for a cover is a separate question. Music business courses, such as Berklee Online's course on revenue streams, treat the mechanical right, the performance right, and synchronization of compositions with video as separate topics, because each one is licensed and paid in a different way.

## A practical checklist for a cover release

Before you release a cover, confirm who owns the song and who administers it. Decide whether you will negotiate directly or use the compulsory license. Keep your arrangement close to the original melody. Get written agreements for everyone who helped make the recording. Ask your distributor what it requires. And plan separately for any video, because audio permission does not automatically extend to it.

## The takeaway

A cover is a new recording of someone else's song. You can own the recording, but the song stays with its writer or publisher, and releasing it takes a license or a direct agreement. Sort out the rights before the release date, not after, and treat video as its own question.
