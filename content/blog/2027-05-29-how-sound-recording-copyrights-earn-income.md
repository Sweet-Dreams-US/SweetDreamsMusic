When you release a song, two different things exist. One is the song itself: the melody and the lyrics. The other is the recording: the actual captured performance that people hear. Each has its own copyright, and each can earn income in its own ways. This post explains the second one, the sound recording copyright, in plain terms.

## Song and recording are different

A song written by a songwriter is a musical work. A recording of that song is a sound recording. They can be owned by different people. A songwriter may own the song while a label, a producer or the artist owns the recording. When artists talk about "the masters," they mean the recordings.

Knowing which one you own is the first step to knowing where your money can come from. If you wrote and recorded your own track and paid for the session, you may own both. If someone else paid for the recording, the ownership may be set by the agreement you signed.

## What the law says a sound recording is

U.S. copyright law defines it. Section 101 of Title 17 says sound recordings are works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds. The definition excludes sounds that accompany a motion picture or other audiovisual work. Section 102 lists sound recordings as one of the categories of work that can be protected by copyright.

:::note Worth knowing
Under Title 17, section 101, a sound recording is a work that results from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds, not counting sounds that accompany a motion picture or other audiovisual work.
:::

## The owner decides how the recording is used

Copyright gives the owner exclusive rights. That means the owner decides who may use the work, and on what terms. When the owner permits someone else to use the recording, that permission is a license. Licensing is the basic way a recording earns money for the person who owns it: other people agree to pay for the right to use it.

For that reason, the paperwork around a recording matters as much as the audio. A clear record of who owns the master, and what has been licensed to whom, is the foundation for every income stream that follows.

## The digital performance right

One of the owner's exclusive rights is specific to recordings. Section 106(6) of the Copyright Act gives the owner of copyright, in the case of sound recordings, the exclusive right to perform the copyrighted work publicly by means of a digital audio transmission.

The law defines a digital transmission as a transmission in whole or in part in a digital or other nonanalog format. This right is why sound recordings have a place in the business of internet and satellite music services. The right covers public performance by digital audio transmission, and the business of exploiting it is a topic in its own right. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams includes a lesson on generating revenue from the exploitation of the digital transmission performance right of a sound recording.

## The Music Modernization Act

In 2018 Congress passed the Music Modernization Act. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that it has three titles.

Title I, the Musical Works Modernization Act, establishes a blanket licensing system for digital phonorecord deliveries. The Copyright Office designated The MLC to collect and distribute mechanical royalty payments under Title I. Note that this title concerns the musical work, the song, and not the recording.

Title II, the Classics Protection and Access Act, gives federal remedies for unauthorized use of sound recordings made before 1972.

Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, concerns recordings directly. Under it, the designated collective, SoundExchange, distributes royalties to producers, mixers and engineers under a letter of direction. This is one place where people who worked on a recording, and not only the performer, can be paid.

## Why this matters to an independent artist

If you make music independently, you may be the songwriter, the performer, the producer and the owner of the recording all at once. Each role can bring a different kind of income, and each may need its own registration or paperwork.

A practical approach is to keep three records. Keep a record of who wrote the song and who owns it. Keep a record of who owns the recording and what was paid for. And keep every license and agreement you sign, including beat licenses, so that you always know what rights you hold.

## Putting it together

A sound recording copyright is a separate right from the song. The owner has exclusive rights, including the right to perform the recording publicly by digital audio transmission. Income comes when the owner licenses the recording or when rights tied to it are exercised and paid out. The Music Modernization Act changed parts of how that money is collected and shared, including for producers, mixers and engineers.
