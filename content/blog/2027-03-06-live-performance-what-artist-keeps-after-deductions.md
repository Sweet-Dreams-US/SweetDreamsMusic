A packed room feels like a win. The crowd sang, the merch table was busy, and you loaded out tired and happy. Then settlement happens and the number on the sheet is smaller than you expected. This is one of the most common surprises for independent artists, and it usually comes down to one thing: the deal was never fully understood before the night.

This article explains the main ways artists get paid for live shows, what the words gross and net really change, and what to check before you sign anything.

## The four common deal structures

Most live deals fall into a few patterns.

A guarantee is a fixed fee paid no matter how many people show up. One booking guide uses a $1,200 guarantee as its example, owed in full at settlement. The promoter carries the risk, because they pay the same amount to the artist whether the room is full or empty.

A door deal pays the artist a percentage of ticket money. Here the artist carries the risk. A good night pays well and a slow night may pay very little. The same guide says that at larger ticketed shows, the artist share of the net over breakeven commonly lands in the 80 to 90 percent range for established headliners. That number is not a rule for every show, and smaller rooms and newer artists often see different terms.

A versus deal pays the greater of a guarantee or a percentage. The guide's example is $4,000 versus 85 percent of net. You collect whichever is higher after the show settles.

A guarantee plus backend pays a fixed fee and then a percentage of revenue above breakeven.

Another source, Music Biz Academy, describes the same patterns with smaller numbers, such as a $300 flat fee, $300 versus 30 percent of the door, or $300 plus 30 percent of the door. It calls payment the single most contested area of a performance deal, which is a good reason to get the terms clear early.

## Gross and net are not the same thing

Gross is the total ticket revenue before any costs come out. Net is the gross minus approved show expenses. According to the booking guide, percentage deals on gross are rare at ticketed shows, and most percentage deals are on net.

That means the question to ask is not only what percentage you get. Ask whether it is gross or net, and what comes out first. The answer can change your pay a great deal.

## What gets deducted

On a net deal, the promoter first recovers a set of approved costs. This total is often called breakeven, or "the nut." Your percentage is calculated only on what is left after that number is covered.

The settlement guide lists common deductions: the venue or facility fee, ticketing and card fees, sound, lights and production, door and security staff, backline rental, agreed advertising, and hospitality or a buyout. The same guide says a deduction that was on the list agreed during the advance is fair, and one that was not on the list is worth asking about.

:::note Worth knowing
Music Biz Academy says percentage payments generally begin after the venue or promoter reaches a break-even point that covers the cost of the show, including promotion and any guarantees made to the artist.
:::

## A worked example

The booking guide gives this illustration. The deal is $4,000 versus 85 percent of net. Tickets cost $25, and the breakeven is $6,000.

With 300 tickets sold, gross is $7,500 and net is $1,500. Eighty-five percent of that is $1,275, so the artist collects the $4,000 guarantee instead.

With 600 tickets sold, gross is $15,000 and net is $9,000. Eighty-five percent of that is $7,650, so the artist collects $7,650.

The guide says these figures are illustrations and not fixed market rates. The point is how the math works. The first deductions reduce the pool the percentage applies to, and the structure of the deal decides which number you are paid.

## Merch and other money from the night

Money made at a show is not only the performance fee. Music Biz Academy says some venues take a percentage of merchandise sales, from 10 to 30 percent, while many smaller venues take none. It also says live CD sales can exceed the performance fee and help cover road expenses.

The settlement guide says venues vary on merch. Some take no cut, some take a percentage, some charge only when house staff sell, and some treat shirts differently from music. Ask about the policy ahead of time and count your stock in and out.

## Commissions and taxes

If a booking agent is involved, they take a commission. One guide mentions a 10 percent commission for booking agents. Music Biz Academy says agents are paid only for the territory where they book shows, not on records, songwriting or publishing, and that union rules cap agent commissions at 10 percent.

On taxes, the settlement guide says that for most domestic shows, settlement usually withholds nothing, and you report and pay your own taxes. Withholding can apply to nonresident or foreign performers. The guide notes this is general information and not tax advice, and the same applies here. A tax professional can answer questions about your own situation.

:::note Worth knowing
Payment timing varies. The settlement guide lists cash on the night, a check handed over at settlement, a deposit plus balance, a bank transfer on agreed terms, or money collected by an agent and passed on to you. Write the timing into the deal.
:::

## A checklist before and after the show

Before the show, ask for these items in writing: whether the percentage is on gross or net, the breakeven number, the full deduction list, who counts the door and how attendance is tracked, and when you will be paid. Music Biz Academy also suggests that established acts may ask for up to 50 percent of their fee up front.

At settlement, check the sheet against these points from the settlement guide. Tickets sold and comps should match the box office report. The gross should use the correct ticket price. Every deduction should have been approved ahead of time. The percentage should be applied to the correct base. The merch cut should be right. The final payout should add up. Keep a signed copy.
