When an artist says "send me the song," they might mean the lyrics and melody, the recording of a performance, or the mp3 sitting in a folder. In everyday talk these blur together. In U.S. copyright law they are separate things, and knowing which one you are holding makes release plans, file handling, and conversations with other people in the business clearer.

## Two things people call the song

The U.S. Copyright Office separates the music from the recording of it. The music and lyrics are a musical work. A recording of someone performing that music is a sound recording. They are two distinct works, and each can be handled on its own.

This post is about the second one and about the object that carries it. The Copyright Office's Circular 56, which covers registration of sound recordings, is the main source here.

## What a sound recording is

Circular 56 describes sound recordings as "works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds." Sounds that accompany a motion picture or other audiovisual work are left out of that definition.

In plain terms, a sound recording is a captured performance, and it is often a performance of another work, such as a song written by someone else or by the same artist. For the sounds to count as a recording, they have to be fixed in a medium from which they can be perceived, reproduced, or communicated.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office says the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics, words, or other underlying content embodied in that recording.
:::

## What a phonorecord is

The recording is the captured sound. The thing that holds it is something else. Circular 56 calls a phonorecord the statutory term for a physical object that contains a sound recording, and it gives a digital audio file, a compact disc, and an LP as examples. The term covers any type of object that may be used to store a sound recording, and the circular names formats such as .mp3 and .wav.

So an mp3 is not the recording. It is a phonorecord that carries the recording. A wav exported from the same session is a second phonorecord of the same recording. The author may fix the sounds in a digital track, a disk, a tape, or another format, and the recording stays the same recording across those carriers.

## Why the difference matters in practice

Here is a simple case. An artist records a song in a session and exports a wav master, an mp3 for sharing, and a version for a streaming upload. That is three files and one recording. If the artist later records the same song again with a different band, that is a second recording, even though the musical work has not changed.

It also explains why a conversation about "the song" can go in two directions at once. One person may be talking about the composition, meaning the music and lyrics. Another may be talking about the recording, meaning that specific captured performance. If a cover of someone else's song is involved, the new recording is a captured performance of an existing musical work. Whether any permission is needed for that is a separate question, and this post does not cover it.

## How the law treats the two sides

The Music Modernization Act shows how the two kinds of work are handled separately. The Copyright Office's page on the act lists three titles.

Title I, the Musical Works Modernization Act, sets up a blanket license for digital music providers that covers downloads and streaming. The Copyright Office designated The MLC to collect and distribute mechanical royalties, and songwriters and publishers had to register with it to receive payments under the new license as of January 1, 2021. This title concerns musical works.

Title II, the Classics Protection and Access Act, brings pre-1972 sound recordings partly into the federal copyright system and gives them federal remedies against unauthorized use. These are recordings fixed before February 15, 1972.

Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets producers, mixers, and sound engineers receive royalties. SoundExchange distributes them under a letter of direction. The page describes these as royalties collected for uses of sound recordings.

:::note Worth knowing
Titles II and III address sound recordings. Title I addresses musical works. The act treats them as two separate subjects.
:::

## Two sources of income

The same split shows up in how music business education describes revenue. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams lists composition income and sound recording income as separate topics. On the composition side it names the performance right and the mechanical reproduction right. On the sound recording side it names the digital transmission performance right. Other lessons cover the synchronization of compositions together with the use of master recordings.

The course page lists topics only. It does not give rates or splits, so this post does not either. The point to take from it is that a release can produce income tied to the song and income tied to the recording, and that the two are tracked separately.

## What to do with this

A few habits follow from the definitions. Keep your exported files labeled by what they carry and which session they came from. Keep track of which version is the final recording and which are copies in other formats. When you talk with a producer, engineer, or collaborator, say whether you mean the song or the recording.
