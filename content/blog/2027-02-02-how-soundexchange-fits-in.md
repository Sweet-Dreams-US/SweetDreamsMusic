A song and a recording of that song are two different things, and they earn money in different ways. The song is the composition: the melody and the lyrics. The recording is the finished sound, the version people actually stream. This article is about one organization that works on the recording side, SoundExchange, and about the paperwork that lets producers and engineers share in what it collects.

If you are an artist, a band member, a producer or an engineer, knowing where SoundExchange fits can save you from leaving money unclaimed or from signing something you do not understand.

## What SoundExchange does

SoundExchange explains digital performance royalties as fees that streaming services are required by law to pay for streaming musical content. Services such as Pandora, SiriusXM and webcasters pay these royalties to SoundExchange, together with playlists of all the recordings they played. SoundExchange then distributes the money to artists and rights owners. The rates are set by the Copyright Royalty Board.

This is a recording-side payment. It is separate from the money paid to songwriters and publishers for the composition, which is handled by other organizations.

:::note Worth knowing
SoundExchange states that services pay these royalties to it along with playlists of all the recordings played, and that it then distributes the money to artists and rights owners.
:::

## The 50, 45 and 5 split

The law divides these digital performance royalties among three groups. According to SoundExchange, 50 percent goes to the copyright owner of the sound recording, which is often a record label or the artist if the artist owns the master. Another 45 percent is paid directly to the featured artists on the recording. The remaining 5 percent goes into a fund for non-featured performers, such as session musicians and backup singers.

One point follows from this. Non-featured performers do not receive royalties directly from SoundExchange, so they are paid through the fund instead.

## Where producers and engineers come in

Producers, mixers and engineers are not part of the split on their own. For many years that left a gap, because these people shape how a recording sounds but were not paid from digital performance royalties.

The Music Modernization Act changed that. According to the U.S. Copyright Office, Title III of the act, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive a share of royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. It sets up a process under which SoundExchange, the designated collective, distributes those royalties according to a letter of direction.

## What a letter of direction is

A letter of direction, often shortened to LOD, is a form. Through it, a featured artist tells SoundExchange to send part of their sound recording performance royalties to a creative participant, such as a producer, engineer, mixer or re-mixer.

SoundExchange sets out several rules for how these letters work:

- The payment is a percentage of the featured artist's share. The letter sets that percentage, and SoundExchange does not give a standard figure.
- SoundExchange does not pay creative participants off the top. They receive only what the letter directs from the featured artist's share.
- If a recording has more than one featured artist, the creative participant needs a letter from each one to be paid on every artist's share.
- Payees must be individuals, or companies solely owned by the creative participant. A letter cannot send money to labels, lenders, royalty advance companies or other parties who were not part of the creative process.
- The performer, or their authorized signatory on file with SoundExchange, signs the letter.
- A letter takes a minimum of two weeks to process.

SoundExchange also notes that payments depend on meeting its payment threshold, and that they pause if a recording's royalties become ineligible, such as during a dispute.

## A practical order of steps

If you are a featured artist, these steps help you stay organized:

1. Keep a clear list of everyone who contributed to each recording, including the producer, mixer and engineer.
2. Agree in writing, before release, what percentage of your share each person will receive.
3. Register with SoundExchange and keep your account details current, so the signatory on file is correct.
4. Submit a letter of direction for each person you agreed to pay, and allow at least two weeks for processing.

If you are a producer or engineer, ask the featured artist about a letter of direction while you are still working on the project. It is much easier to settle at the start than after the recording has been out for months.

## What this article does not cover

SoundExchange handles digital performance royalties on the recording side. It does not collect royalties for songwriters and publishers, and the terms in a particular deal can vary. This article is general information and not legal advice. For your own agreements, talk with a music attorney.
