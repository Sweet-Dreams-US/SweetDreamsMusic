When a song is finished, the artist is usually the name people see. The producer, the mixer, and the engineer shaped how it sounds, and they have a stake in how it earns. Artists who work with studio professionals, and studio professionals themselves, often ask the same question: who pays whom, and when?

This article covers one part of that answer. It explains a United States law that gives producers, mixers, and engineers a direct way to collect certain digital royalties. It also lists the questions worth settling in writing before a session is over. This is general information, not legal advice.

## Why the question comes up

A recording is made by several people. The artist sings or plays, a producer guides the sound, an engineer captures it, and a mixer balances it. Each person's work is part of the finished track, and each person may expect to be credited and paid for it. Those expectations are easiest to meet when they are discussed early.

Money from a recording does not arrive in one lump. It comes from different uses of the music, and each use has its own rules. One of those uses is non-interactive digital radio, where a listener hears a stream but does not choose the exact song. The rest of this article is about royalties from that kind of use.

## How it worked before the AMP Act

The Recording Academy explains that featured performers have held a statutory right to 45 percent of non-interactive digital performance royalties since 1995. Before the AMP Act, producers and engineers who wanted a share had to collect it from the featured performers' portion.

That happened under a voluntary SoundExchange policy. The Recording Academy describes that policy as neither permanent nor secure. A studio professional depended on a policy that could change.

## What the AMP Act changed

The Music Modernization Act includes Title III, also called the Allocation for Music Producers Act, or AMP Act. The U.S. Copyright Office says it lets music producers, mixers, and sound engineers who helped create a sound recording, but who do not receive section 114 royalties by statute, be paid from section 114 statutory license royalties.

The law sets up a process through SoundExchange, the designated collective. SoundExchange distributes these royalties under a document called a letter of direction. The Recording Academy describes the result as a legal and permanent right to collect non-interactive digital royalties directly, when a letter of direction with the featured artist provides for it.

:::note In short
Under the AMP Act, a producer, mixer, or engineer can be paid directly by SoundExchange when the featured artist signs a letter of direction.
:::

## The letter of direction

According to the Copyright Office FAQ, a letter of direction comes from an authorized artist payee. It directs SoundExchange to pay a portion of the royalties directly to the producer, mixer, or engineer.

The sources reviewed for this article do not set a fixed share for studio professionals. The portion is a matter for the people on the record to agree on. That is why the paperwork matters. For most recordings, direct payment depends on that letter, so an unsigned letter can mean a missed payment route.

The Recording Academy says that for most recordings, the artist's written agreement is what lets a studio professional collect directly.

## What the law does not cover

The royalty right described here is limited to non-interactive digital services. It does not reach interactive services, where a listener picks a specific song on demand.

Older recordings are handled differently. The Copyright Office says the AMP Act directs SoundExchange to adopt a policy for cases without a letter of direction. Under that policy, royalties for certain recordings made before 1995 can be paid to producers, mixers, or engineers from the featured artist's share, if the artist payee is notified and does not object.

This article covers only the AMP Act route. Producers and engineers may have other payment arrangements with an artist, and those are outside its scope. Whatever the arrangement, it should be written down.

## What to settle in writing

The law gives the mechanism. The people on a project still have to use it. A few questions are worth answering before a session ends.

Who is the authorized artist payee on the recording? Will a letter of direction be signed, and when? What portion goes to the producer, to the mixer, and to the engineer? Is the person paid a flat fee, a share of royalties, or both? A short written agreement that answers these questions prevents a long argument later.

If the amounts are large or the situation is unusual, a music attorney can review the paperwork. That is an ordinary step, not a sign of distrust.
