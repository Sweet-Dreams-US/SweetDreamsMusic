If you write songs and they stream in the United States, there is a royalty that does not simply show up in your account. A songwriter has to sign up to receive it, and the place to sign up is The Mechanical Licensing Collective, usually called The MLC. This post explains what The MLC is, why it exists, and where registering fits when you are getting ready to release music.

## Two kinds of rights in every song

Every recorded song carries two separate sets of rights. One belongs to the recording, which is the specific performance captured in the studio. The other belongs to the musical work, which is the composition: the melody and the lyrics. The two earn money through different channels, and they often have different owners.

An artist who writes and records their own song usually holds both sets of rights, but the money for each one still arrives through a different system. That is why an artist can have a song streaming everywhere and still be missing a payment they did not know to ask for.

The Music Modernization Act deals with both sides. According to the U.S. Copyright Office, Title I covers musical works, and Title III lets producers, mixers, and sound engineers receive royalties through SoundExchange. The MLC belongs to Title I, so it is about the composition side of your song.

## What the Music Modernization Act changed

Title I created a blanket licensing system. Under it, digital music providers can make and distribute digital phonorecord deliveries, which the Copyright Office lists as permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams. In plain terms, a streaming service can work under one blanket license instead of tracking down every songwriter and publisher one at a time.

The law also needed someone to collect the money and pay it out. The Copyright Office designated The MLC for that job. The MLC's own site says it administers blanket mechanical licenses for eligible U.S. streaming and download services, collects the royalties due under those licenses, and pays songwriters, composers, lyricists, music publishers, administrators, and collective management organizations.

## Why registration is on you

The Copyright Office states that as of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC to receive royalty payments under the blanket license. The word that matters is must. Registration is how your songs get matched to you. A service reporting a large number of plays cannot work out who wrote a song from the title alone, so the writer's information has to be on file.

## Who should register

The MLC's site says that anyone entitled to receive digital audio mechanical royalties for the use of their music in the U.S. should become a Member. It names songwriters, composers, lyricists, music publishers, and administrators among those it pays. It also says it pays self-administered songwriters, which covers the artist who writes their own songs and has no publisher.

If you do have a publisher or an administrator, do not assume the registration is done. Ask them directly whether they register your songs with The MLC, and ask how you can confirm that your works are on file. A short, direct question now is much easier than finding a gap later.

## What registering looks like

Members register their songs in the Member Hub, which is part of The MLC Portal. The MLC's site says it distributes royalties to Members monthly, and that streaming and download services report usage and royalties to it each month.

A practical habit is to keep one simple list of every song you have written. For each song, write down the title, the co-writers, and each person's share. Registering a work means giving this kind of information, and a disagreement between co-writers about shares is far easier to settle before a song is released than after money is moving. Settle the splits first, then register.

## What about royalties no one has claimed

The MLC's site also refers to unclaimed accrued royalties. It describes plans to distribute remaining unclaimed accrued blanket royalties by market share, and it lists dates for those distributions. Dates and rules for that process can change, so read The MLC's current notices instead of relying on a blog post.

The practical point for a songwriter is simple. Registering is the step that connects your songs to your name. Waiting leaves your works unmatched while the plays keep adding up.

## Where registration fits in a release

A release has a lot of moving parts: the song, the cover art, the video, short clips for social platforms, and a calendar of posts. Registration is an administrative task that is easy to forget because it produces nothing anyone can see. That is the reason to write it down as a dated item on the same checklist as everything else.

A sensible order looks like this. First, finish the song and agree on the writers and their shares. Second, register the song with The MLC. Third, set the release date and build the rollout around it. Doing the paperwork before the date means the release day is about the music and not about fixing records.

## What The MLC does not cover

Keep the scope clear. The MLC's site describes its job as administering blanket mechanical licenses for U.S. streaming and download services and paying out the digital audio mechanical royalties from them. It does not describe itself as the place to collect every other kind of music income. Producers, mixers, and engineers have their own route through SoundExchange under Title III, as the Copyright Office explains.

Registering with The MLC is one piece of a larger picture. It is a piece worth doing early, because it is the one that depends entirely on you.
