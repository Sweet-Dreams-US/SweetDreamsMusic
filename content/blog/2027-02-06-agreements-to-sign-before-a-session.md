When a session goes well, nobody thinks about paperwork. The song sounds right, the takes are good, and everyone leaves happy. Questions about who owns the recording tend to show up later, when the track is about to be released, licensed or sold. By then it is much harder to settle them.

This article explains why artists settle ownership of a recording in writing before work begins, and what to cover. It is general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, talk with a music attorney.

## A recording and a song are two things

Before the agreement, it helps to separate two things that people often treat as one.

A song is the composition: the melody, the lyrics, the structure. A recording is a particular captured performance of that song. The U.S. Copyright Office describes a sound recording as "a recorded performance, often of another work," and says the copyright in the recording covers the recording itself, not the underlying song or lyrics.

:::note What a sound recording is
The U.S. Copyright Office's Circular 56 describes a sound recording as a work that results from fixing sounds in a medium such as a digital track, disc or tape. It says the copyright covers the recording, not the songs, lyrics or text underneath it.
:::

This matters because each one can have different owners. You may write a song alone and record it with a producer. The song and the recording are then separate questions, and each needs an answer.

## Who is the author by default

If nothing is written down, the law has a starting point. Circular 56 says the authors of a sound recording are the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds.

That means a producer or engineer who does creative work on a session can be an author of the recording, even if the artist paid for the session and thinks of it as theirs. The default is a starting point. It is the reason a written agreement is worth having.

## What "work made for hire" means

The circular describes two situations where the author is someone other than the performer or producer.

The first is employment. If a performer or producer makes the recording as part of a typical employment relationship, the recording is a work made for hire and the employer is the author.

The second is a commissioned recording. The circular says that when a performer or producer makes a recording for a third party in certain categories of work, and the parties agree in writing that it is a work made for hire, the third party is the author.

:::note Writing is the point
Circular 56 says that for commissioned work to count as a work made for hire, the parties must agree in writing. A verbal understanding at the start of a session does not meet that description.
:::

Whether a particular recording fits those rules is a legal question. The practical lesson from the circular is simple: where ownership is meant to be different from the default, it is put in writing.

## What to settle before the session

Every project is different, so the exact terms depend on the people involved. These are the questions most artists want answered before work begins:

- Who will own the recording when it is finished?
- Is the producer or engineer being paid a flat fee, a share of royalties, or both?
- Can the artist release, license and sell the recording without asking anyone?
- Will the producer or engineer be credited, and how?
- What happens to the session files after the project is done?
- If more than one person is performing, how are the shares divided?

Writing these down does two things. It makes each person say what they expect. And it gives everyone something to point to if memories differ later.

## Why royalties make this more than a formality

Money from a recording does not stop at the first sale. The U.S. Copyright Office's page on the Music Modernization Act says that one part of the law covers royalties collected for uses of sound recordings, and that producers, mixers and engineers can receive them. Another part sets up a blanket licensing system for digital music providers.

:::note Who can be paid
The Copyright Office's summary of the Music Modernization Act says Title III covers royalties collected for uses of sound recordings, which producers, mixers and engineers can receive.
:::

The agreement before the session is where the artist and the producer decide how these payments are handled. Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams lists featured artist and producer advances and royalties, and copyright licensing, ownership and revenue sharing, as subjects in its curriculum. These are standard topics because they come up on real projects.

## Keep it simple and keep a copy

An agreement does not have to be long to be useful. It needs to name the people, describe the project, say who owns the recording, say how everyone is paid, and be signed by the people it affects. Each person should have a copy.

If the project is bigger, such as an album, a release with several producers, or a recording that will be licensed for other uses, it is worth having a music attorney review the terms. This article cannot tell you what your agreement should say.

## Before you book a session

A short list to check:

- You know who will be in the room and what each person is doing.
- You know whether anyone besides you will have a claim on the recording.
- The terms are written down and signed before the first take.
- Everyone has a copy.
