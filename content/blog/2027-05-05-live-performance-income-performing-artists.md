For many artists who perform, the stage is where much of the money is made. That is a claim worth looking at closely, because it shapes how a performer spends time and money before a release, between releases, and on the road. This article explains what a music business course from Berklee Online says about live performance as income, how the money moves between the people involved, and what an artist can prepare to make a live show easier to book and easier to promote.

## What the source says

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams says that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. The course description adds that artists increasingly need other, or ancillary, income to get by.

One lesson in the course is titled "The Live Music Industry: Primary Breadwinner for Performing Artists." The course page gives no figures or percentages for touring income, so this article does not either. The point it makes is about the shape of an artist's income: for performers, live work is a main source, and other sources fill in around it.

:::

## Gross and net are different numbers

The same course includes a lesson called "Get Paid, Not Played," which covers how artists are compensated for live performances, including compensation issues and net earnings.

The word net matters. What a show brings in and what the performer keeps are not the same number. The course maps how live revenue flows among the people involved: performers, managers, agents, promoters, and venues. Each of them has a role in a show, and the course treats the flow of money between them as something an artist needs to understand.

For an artist planning a year, this means the useful question about a show is not only how much it pays. It is how much reaches the artist after the other parties in the chain are accounted for. The course page does not give typical splits, so no numbers are offered here. An artist should read any offer or agreement closely and ask what the final payment to the artist will be.

## Live income carries event risk

The Berklee course also includes an assignment on how unruly crowds affect the festival scene. The course page does not say more than that, but the topic suggests that live income depends on events going well, and events can be disrupted.

That is a reason to treat live work as one part of an artist's plan and not the only part. The course description says as much when it notes that artists increasingly need ancillary income.

## Questions to ask before you say yes to a show

Because the source stresses net earnings and the number of parties in the chain, a few questions are worth asking before an artist accepts a date. Ask who is paying the artist and when. Ask what the artist's final payment will be and what, if anything, comes out of it. Ask who else is involved, whether that is a manager, an agent, a promoter, or the venue, and what each of them is paid. Ask what happens if the event is changed or cancelled.

None of these questions are rude. They are part of understanding how the money moves, which is the subject of the lesson the source titles "Get Paid, Not Played."

## The short version

For performers, live work is described by Berklee Online as the bulk of artist revenue, and as a primary breadwinner. The same source says artists increasingly need ancillary income as well. The money in live work moves through several parties, so net earnings are the number to understand. An artist can prepare for live work with footage, photos, and short clips, and can plan those alongside a release.
