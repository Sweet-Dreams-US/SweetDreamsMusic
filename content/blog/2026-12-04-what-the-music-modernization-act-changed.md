If you make music and release it, you have probably heard people mention the Music Modernization Act, sometimes shortened to the MMA. It became law in the United States, and the U.S. Copyright Office calls it "the most significant piece of copyright legislation in decades." That sounds large and distant. In practice, it changed who collects certain royalties, how some old recordings are protected, and whether the people who engineered a record can be paid.

This article walks through the three parts of the act in plain terms. It is general information about how the law is set up. It is not legal advice, and your own situation may need a music attorney.

## Why the act was needed

Before the act, the compulsory license that lets a streaming or download service use a song was structured song by song. The act replaced that structure for digital deliveries, and it also dealt with older recordings and with the people who produce and engineer records.

The act has three titles, which are sections of the law. Each one deals with a different kind of problem. It helps to keep them apart, because they affect different people.

## Title I: the blanket license for songs

Title I covers the musical work, which means the song itself: the melody and lyrics, owned by the songwriter, composer or publisher. It is separate from the recording of that song.

The Copyright Office describes Title I as replacing the song by song compulsory licensing structure for musical works with a blanket license covering digital music deliveries, such as downloads and streams. A blanket license covers many works at once, so a service does not need a separate license for each song.

To run that license, the law set up a body called the Mechanical Licensing Collective, or MLC. On July 8, 2019, the Copyright Office designated Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. to do this work. The Recording Academy describes the MLC as a nonprofit that administers the blanket mechanical license for streaming and download services. It collects mechanical royalties from those services and distributes them to songwriters, composers, lyricists and music publishers.

The same source gives January 1, 2021 as the date the blanket license started.

:::note Worth knowing
Per the Recording Academy, music creators must register their songs with the MLC to get paid. Publishers and self-administered songwriters, composers and lyricists also need to register with the MLC to see their data in its portal.
:::

The practical point for an independent songwriter is that the money does not find you on its own. If you write songs and have never registered them, check whether your works are registered. If you have a publisher or an administrator, ask them whether they have done it for you.

## Title II: older recordings

Title II is called the Classics Protection and Access Act. It deals with sound recordings, which are the recorded performances, as opposed to the songs.

Recordings made before February 15, 1972 had been outside the federal copyright system in the United States. Title II brought them in, in part, by creating a new chapter 14 in Title 17 of the U.S. Code. The Copyright Office says it extends infringement remedies to the owners of recordings fixed before that date.

If you are a newer artist, this title may not touch your own releases. It does matter if you sample, reissue or license older material, or if you work with a catalog that includes older masters. In those cases, the age of the recording is one of the first things to check.

## Title III: producers, mixers and engineers

Title III is called the Allocation for Music Producers Act. It is the part of the act that matters most to the people behind the console.

The Copyright Office says Title III allows music producers, mixers and sound engineers to receive royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license. SoundExchange distributes those royalties under what the law calls a "letter of direction."

In plain terms, a producer or engineer can ask to be paid a share of certain digital performance royalties directly, instead of relying only on a flat fee or on the artist passing money along. The letter of direction is the document that tells SoundExchange who should receive that share.

:::note Worth knowing
This applies to royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license, which is one specific source of money. It does not mean a producer is automatically paid from every stream or sale.
:::

If you hire a producer or engineer, agree on this before the session, not after the release. A short written agreement that names the split and says whether a letter of direction will be filed keeps both sides clear. If you are the producer or engineer, ask about it early.

## What to do with this as an artist

The three titles come down to a few habits that are worth building before you release music.

First, register your songs. Title I only works for you if the collective knows the work exists and who owns it.

Second, keep your credits accurate. Royalties follow credits. A producer, mixer or engineer who is not listed correctly is harder to pay, and a songwriter who is missing from a split can cause delays for everyone.

Third, write down your agreements. Who wrote what, who owns what, and who is owed what should exist on paper before a song goes live.

Fourth, plan the release so the paperwork is done before the date. Registration and credits are easier to fix before a song is public than after it has spread across services.
