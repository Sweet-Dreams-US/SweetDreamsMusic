When you release a track, you are releasing two things at once. One is the song. The other is the recording of that song. They sound like one thing to a listener, but under U.S. copyright law they are two separate works, and each can have different owners, different agreements, and different ways of getting paid.

This post explains the difference in plain terms, so you know which questions to ask before you record, release, or license anything.

## The song is the musical work

The song is the music and the lyrics. The U.S. Copyright Office puts it this way in its guidance on sound recordings: the underlying music and lyrics are a "musical work." This is the composition. It exists whether or not anyone ever records it. A melody and words written on paper, or played once on a guitar, are already the song.

When people say "song copyright," they usually mean the copyright in the musical work. Who owns it depends on who wrote it and on any agreements the writers have signed.

## The master is the sound recording

A recording is a different work. The Copyright Office defines sound recordings as works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds. In everyday music talk, the finished recording is called the master.

The Copyright Office is clear about what the copyright in a recording covers. It covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics, or other underlying content captured in that recording. That content belongs to the musical work.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office says the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself, and does not cover the music, lyrics, words, or other underlying content embodied in that recording.
:::

## One song, many recordings

The easiest way to see that these are separate works is to think about a cover. If another artist records a song that you wrote, there is one song and two recordings. The Copyright Office gives this kind of example in its circular: a song and a separate recording of that same song by another artist are two distinct works.

This also works in the other direction. You may write a song and then record several versions of it, such as a studio version, an acoustic version, and a live version. Each recording is its own sound recording, and all of them share the same underlying song.

## Who is the author of each

For the song, the authors are the writers of the music and lyrics. For the recording, the rules are different. The Copyright Office says the author of a sound recording is the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds.

There are two important exceptions. If the recording was made by an employee as part of the job, the employer is the author. If it was made for a third party under a written work-made-for-hire agreement, the third party is the author. This is why the paperwork before a session matters. Who ends up owning a master can depend on what was signed.

## A recording is not the same as a file

Another term you will see is "phonorecord." The Copyright Office describes it as the statutory term for a physical object that contains a sound recording, and says the term includes digital formats such as .mp3 and .wav files.

So a sound recording is the content, and a phonorecord is the object or file that holds it. When you send someone an mp3, you are sending a copy of the recording. You are not handing over ownership of it.

## Why the money is separate too

Because there are two works, there are two sets of rights. The Berklee Online course on music business revenue streams treats them separately. It lists composition rights, such as the performance right and the mechanical reproduction right, apart from rights in the sound recording, such as the digital transmission performance right. It also covers the synchronization of compositions and the use of master recordings in television, film, commercials, and video games as its own topic.

The Music Modernization Act shows the same split in how royalties are handled. For songs, it replaced the song-by-song licensing system for digital delivery with a blanket license for digital music providers, covering downloads and streams. On July 8, 2019, the Copyright Office named Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. as the designated Mechanical Licensing Coordinator. For recordings, the law describes SoundExchange as the designated collective that distributes royalties collected for uses of sound recordings under the section 114 statutory license. SoundExchange can pay producers, mixers, and sound engineers through a letter of direction.

In short, the song and the recording each have their own path to payment, and different organizations handle each.

## Why this matters before you release

Knowing the split changes the questions you ask. Before a session, ask who owns the recording when it is finished, and get the answer in writing. Before you release, ask who wrote the song and whether the credits are accurate. Before you cover another writer's song, remember that your new recording and the song are separate works, so the song's writer still matters.

## A short checklist

Write down who wrote the music and who wrote the lyrics. Write down who performed and who produced the recording. Put ownership of the recording in a signed agreement before the session. Keep a copy of every agreement for every track.
