A song is often made quickly, in a room with several people, and the details of who did what are easy to forget a month later. Writing them down on the day the song is made costs a few minutes. Not having them can cause a disagreement later, when the song is being released or used.

This article explains what the U.S. Copyright Office says about the song and the recording, who counts as an author of each, and what a simple record should contain. It is general information and not legal advice.

## A song and a recording are two works

Copyright treats the music and lyrics as one work and the recording of them as another. The Copyright Office calls the first a musical work. Its guidance on sound recordings uses the Adele song "Rolling in the Deep" as an example, saying the musical work is authored by Adele and Paul Epworth.

The recording is a sound recording. The Copyright Act defines sound recordings as works that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken or other sounds. So the notes and words belong to the first work, and the captured performance belongs to the second.

This matters for your record, because the people who wrote the song and the people who made the recording can be different. A writer who was not in the studio can still be an author of the musical work.

## Who is an author of the recording

The Copyright Office says the author of a sound recording is the performer featured in the recording and the producer who captured and processed the sounds that appear in the final recording. If the performer is also the producer, one person is both.

There is an exception for work done for someone else. If an employee makes the recording within a typical employment relationship, it is a work made for hire and the employer is the author. If a performer or producer makes the recording for a third party, that third party is the author only if the parties agreed in writing that the recording is a work made for hire.

:::note Worth knowing
Copyright protection in the United States exists automatically from the moment an original work of authorship is fixed. Registration is optional, but it creates a public record of facts about the work's authorship and ownership, and a U.S. work must be registered before it can be enforced through litigation.
:::

## What joint authorship means

The Copyright Office describes a joint work as one where two or more authors create a single work with the intent of merging their contributions into inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole. In that case the authors are joint authors and have an indivisible interest in the work as a whole.

Its example in the sound recording guidance is two people who co-create five unpublished recordings and co-own the copyright. Each joint author holds an interest in the entire work, not in a separate portion of it.

The Copyright Office material does not set out how co-owners should divide money or decisions. It does say that transfers of copyright generally must be in writing and signed by the owner. For that reason, an agreement that is spoken in the room and not recorded is a weak foundation.

## What to write down

A useful record is short. Write it on the day of the session, and have everyone who contributed read it and agree to it.

1. The title of the song and the date it was made.
2. The full legal name of each person who wrote music or lyrics, and what each one wrote.
3. The performers on the recording and the person who produced it.
4. Anything that was borrowed, such as a sample or a part from an earlier song.
5. Any agreement about ownership or splits, in writing, with each person's signature or written confirmation.

The record does not decide who owns what. The law and your agreements do that. What the record does is show what everyone remembered and agreed to at the time.

## Credits for producers, mixers and engineers

The Music Modernization Act has a section that addresses people who work on the recording side. The Copyright Office says Title III of the Act allows music producers, mixers or sound engineers to receive royalties. SoundExchange pays these royalties to such parties through a letter of direction.

For your records, this means that the producer, mixer and engineer should be listed by name with the rest of the credits. If one of them will be paid through a letter of direction, that is something to settle with them and put in writing before release.

## Why credits matter at release

When a recording is released, the names on it go to streaming services, to the artist's page and to anyone writing about the release. Wrong names are hard to fix once they are spread across services. A written record makes the first submission correct.

Sweet Dreams Music's site says that every episode of its live series The Sweet Spot goes up on the Sweet Dreams YouTube channel with full credit links. That is one example of a place where credits appear in public, so the names need to be right before the video goes out.

## A habit to keep

Make the record part of every session, the same way you would note the key or the tempo. Keep one copy with the project files and give a copy to each person who is listed. If you are unsure what an agreement should say, ask a music attorney. This article cannot decide that for you.
