Most artists do not live on one source of income. Streams, shows and merchandise get the attention, but they are rarely the whole picture. This article covers three other sources: teaching, grants and crowdfunding. For each one it explains what it is, who it suits and what to check before you plan around it.

## Why artists use more than one income source

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams states that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. It also says artists need ancillary income sources, meaning money that comes from places other than the main activity. The course covers crowdfunding, grants, residencies and prizes, and teaching as separate lessons, and it treats combining live performance, branding, copyrights and recorded music as a strategy.

:::note Worth knowing
Berklee Online lists crowdfunding, grants, residencies, prizes and teaching as revenue sources that sit alongside live performance and recorded music.
:::

The point is not that every artist should do all of these. The point is that income from one source can drop, and a second or third source gives you room when that happens.

## Teaching

Teaching means passing on what you know. That can be private lessons, group classes, workshops, or work at a school or community program. Berklee describes teaching as revenue from educating the next generation of musicians.

Teaching has some practical strengths. The pay is usually predictable compared with show income, because lessons and classes follow a schedule. It also builds skills that help your own work, since explaining a technique makes you examine it. The tradeoff is time. Every hour you teach is an hour you are not writing, recording or rehearsing, so decide how many hours you can give before you commit.

Before you start, think about three things. Decide who your students are, such as beginners, teens or working musicians. Decide where you will teach and whether you need a space. Decide what you will charge, and check what other teachers in your area charge so your price is realistic.

## Grants

A grant is money given for a stated purpose that you do not repay. Grants can fund a recording, a tour, a residency or a community project. They are competitive, and each one has rules about who can apply and what it will pay for.

Read the rules first. The National Endowment for the Arts lists direct grants to individuals among the things its project funding does not cover, and it asks applicant organizations to involve individual artists in their projects. That means an individual artist may not be able to apply to that program on their own, and may instead need to work with an eligible organization that applies. Other funders have different rules, so check each one.

:::note Worth knowing
The National Endowment for the Arts states that its funding does not include direct grants to individuals, and it encourages applicant organizations to involve individual artists in their projects.
:::

A few habits help with grants. Keep a calendar of deadlines, because late applications are usually not considered. Write the project plan in plain terms, with a clear budget and a clear result. Keep samples of your work, such as recordings and videos, ready to send, because most applications ask for them.

## Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding means asking a group of supporters to fund a project, usually in exchange for something they receive. On Kickstarter, funding is all or nothing. Kickstarter's help center states that no one is charged for a pledge toward a project unless it reaches its funding goal.

That rule shapes how you plan. If you set a goal too high and miss it, you receive nothing. If you set it too low, you may reach it and still not have enough to deliver what you promised. Work out your real costs first, including making and shipping any rewards and any platform fees, then set the goal from that number.

Campaigns also depend on people who already want to hear from you. Most backers are existing fans, friends and family, not strangers. Before you launch, build a list of people who will back you and tell them the date.

## What to check before you count on any of these

Whichever source you choose, run the same checks. Ask how much money it could realistically bring in and by when. Ask how many hours it will take and what you will give up to do it. Ask whether the money comes with conditions, such as a deadline, a deliverable or a report. Ask what happens if it does not work out and whether you can cover your costs anyway.

Taxes and legal terms differ by situation, and this article is general education, not tax or legal advice. If you are unsure how a grant or campaign income will be treated, ask a qualified accountant.

## Putting it together

A simple plan might use one steady source and one project source. For example, you might teach a few hours a week for predictable income and run a crowdfunding campaign or apply for a grant when you have a specific project. Start small, track what each source actually pays, and keep the ones that earn their time.
