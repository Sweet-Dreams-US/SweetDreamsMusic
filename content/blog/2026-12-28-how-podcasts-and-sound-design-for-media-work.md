Not all audio work is a song. Podcasts, video soundtracks, trailers and branded content all need recorded sound that is edited, balanced and sometimes scored. Musicians and producers often meet this work when someone asks them to make a show sound better, or to supply music for a video. This article explains how that work is organized and what the main ideas are.

## Two jobs that overlap

Podcast editing and sound design are related, and they are not the same job. Editing is about the spoken recording: cutting, tightening and putting segments in order. Sound design is about the sonic world around the voices: music, ambience and effects.

A small show may have one person doing both. A larger production may split them. Knowing which job you are doing helps you plan the time it takes.

## How podcast editing works

Georgia Tech’s library guide on audio production describes a simple workflow. Give your editor an outline or storyboard so they can make a rough cut for the team to review before the final version. Listen to the audio several times while cutting, and remove distracting filler words. The guide notes that in Audacity you can handle "ums, uhs, and pauses" much like text in a word processor.

The same guide offers a rule worth remembering: "Every editing decision should have a clear purpose, enriching the listener’s experience." A cut should make the audio clearer or shorter for a reason, not only because a cut was possible.

## Record well so editing is easier

Editing starts at the recording. The Georgia Tech guide recommends running soundchecks: record test audio, listen back, and adjust microphone placement. It also says to test your audio levels before you begin. Audacity shows sound as a waveform, where louder sounds appear as larger waves, which makes problems with levels easy to see.

The guide also suggests recording about a minute of ambient sound, called room tone, if possible. Room tone is the sound of the space when nobody is speaking. Laying it under a cut helps cover the edit so the listener does not notice a gap.

:::

## What sound design is

The Trinity College podcasting resource describes sound design as "the craft of creating an overall sonic palette for a piece of art." It says sound design can include voice over, ambiance, foley sounds, audio effects, music and silence. Silence is on the list because a pause is a choice just like a sound.

The same page offers questions for planning. What point of view should the story convey, and how can sound express it? How should the listener feel? Is location important, and how will it be conveyed? How can sound expand or contract the listener’s sense of time? Working through questions like these before you open the editing software makes the choices easier to defend later.

## How sound design shapes attention

In an interview posted by Columbia University Libraries, a podcast sound designer says he sees scoring and sound design as "tools to shape a listener’s attention." The goal is to help listeners become immersed in important moments, and he describes the principle as "show, don’t tell."

He lists the elements he places on a timeline:

- Narration and dialogue, which carry the story.
- Guest tape and archival audio, used with ambience to smooth edits.
- Music, used to support the emotions the listener is meant to feel.
- Ambience that matches the location, such as crowd noise for a train station scene.
- Effects, where each action has an associated sound, such as footsteps or a door.

He also gives levels and mixing advice. Background elements should stay subdued so they do not compete with the voices. Sounds can be panned to match where they come from, and effects can be sent through a shared reverb so they seem to happen in the same space. Effects should line up with what the narration describes, and ambience can fade in and out at story transitions.

## It takes time

Sound design is slow work. The same designer estimates that about two minutes of sound design takes somewhere between two and five hours. That figure comes from one person’s process and one kind of show, so it is not a standard rate. It does show why sound design is planned and budgeted, instead of added at the end.

## Licensing is part of the work

Audio from other sources raises rights questions. The Columbia interview says fair use does not let an institution simply use archival audio or anything unlicensed, and that effects should come from licensed libraries. The same idea applies to music: if you did not make it or license it, check before you use it.

Berklee Online’s course on music business revenue streams lists the synchronization of compositions and master recordings for television, film, commercials and video games as its own topic. That is a reminder that music placed with moving pictures, and by extension other media, is a licensing matter as well as a creative one. Keep a record of where each piece of music and each effect came from.

## A planning checklist

Before you start a podcast or a media audio project, work through these points:

- Write an outline so the editor knows the shape of the piece.
- Do a soundcheck and test levels before recording.
- Record room tone in each space you use.
- Decide what the sound should make the listener feel.
- Keep music and effects below the voice.
- Use only music and effects you made or have a license for.
- Keep notes on sources so you can prove where each sound came from.
