When a label or publisher offers money up front, the number is easy to see and the terms are easy to miss. An advance and royalties are two parts of the same arrangement, and you cannot judge one without the other. This article explains how they fit together so you can read an offer with clear eyes.

## What an advance is

An advance is a sum of money paid to an artist on account of future royalties. In a recording contract it is usually paid when you sign, and it can be paid again each time the label exercises an option for another album or period.

The key phrase is "on account of future royalties." The money is not a gift and it is not a salary. It is your own future earnings, paid early. That one idea explains almost everything else in this article.

## How recoupment works

Recoupment is the process by which the label recovers the advance from your royalty income first. Until the advance is paid back that way, the royalties you earn go toward the advance instead of into your pocket.

Sound On Sound's guide to recording contracts notes that before an artist is paid royalties, the label will also recoup recording costs and usually 50 percent of video costs. In many deals the advance is part of a recording fund, and the whole amount is recoupable. If any of the fund is left over after the recording is finished, that surplus goes to the artist.

Here is a simple way to think about it with made-up numbers. Say an advance is 20,000 dollars. Your royalties on sales and streams add up to 5,000 dollars in the first year. You receive nothing extra that year, and 15,000 dollars is still unrecouped. Only after royalties have covered the full recoupable amount do payments start reaching you.

## Is an advance a loan you have to repay?

It depends on the wording of the contract, and this is one of the most important points to check. The same guide advises avoiding any wording that makes the advance repayable. If it is repayable, it can become a personal debt. The safer arrangement is one where you only "repay" the advance out of royalties, so that if the music does not earn enough, the label bears the loss.

This is a point to raise with an entertainment lawyer before signing. Two contracts with the same advance can leave you in very different positions depending on this one clause.

## How royalties are worked out

Royalties are a percentage of a price or of income. Sound On Sound says that in a typical major-label deal an artist earns somewhere between 14 and 18 percent of the record's dealer price. Deals differ, so treat that as a reference point and not a promise about any offer you receive.

The effective rate is usually lower than the headline rate because of deductions. The guide lists a packaging deduction of 20 to 25 percent on CDs, reduced rates for foreign sales, budget records, record clubs and TV-advertised albums, and no royalty at all on free goods. Labels also commonly hold back about 10 percent as a reserve for returns. A producer's royalty usually comes out of the artist's share, so a 3 percent producer royalty on a 15 percent artist rate leaves the artist with 12 percent before other deductions.

:::note Worth knowing
Sound On Sound reports that a typical major-label artist royalty is between 14 and 18 percent of the record's dealer price, and that standard deductions reduce the effective rate.
:::

## Songwriting income is separate

Money from the recording is one stream. Money from the song itself is another, and it is paid differently. The Music Modernization Act changed how songwriters and publishers get paid when digital services use their songs. Title I created a blanket licence, so a digital service can make and distribute downloads and interactive streams under one licence instead of licensing each song separately.

The U.S. Copyright Office designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, to collect and distribute those mechanical royalties. As of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers must register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the blanket licence. If you write your own songs, make sure your works are registered.

The same law has a part for the people who make the recording. Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties through SoundExchange.

:::note Worth knowing
Berklee Online describes live music as continuing to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists, and says the digital age has pushed artists to build other income sources.
:::

## Questions to ask before you sign

Bring a short list to the table. Ask what exactly is recoupable: recording costs, video costs, tour support, marketing. Ask whether the advance is repayable if royalties never cover it. Ask what the royalty rate is after deductions, not before. Ask whether larger advances are available if the label exercises later options. Sound On Sound recommends negotiating for those whether or not earlier advances have been recouped.

Also ask who handles your songwriting registration with The MLC, and who is responsible for the producer's share. Clear answers now prevent surprises on a statement two years later.

## The short version

An advance is future royalties paid early. Recoupment is how the label gets that money back first. Your royalty rate, after deductions, decides how long that takes, and the contract wording decides what happens if it never does. Get the terms in writing, ask for them in plain numbers, and have a lawyer who works in music read the contract.
