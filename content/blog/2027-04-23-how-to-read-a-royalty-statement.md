A royalty statement can look like a wall of numbers. Titles, territories, units, rates and amounts run across the page, and it is easy to look only at the total at the bottom. This article walks through how to read a statement line by line, and explains why an artist or songwriter can receive several different statements for the same song.

## Why one song can produce more than one statement

A song involves two kinds of copyright: the musical work, which is the composition, and the sound recording, which is the specific recording of it. Different organizations collect money for each, so a single release can produce statements from several places.

The Copyright Alliance describes the main types. A mechanical royalty is paid for reproducing and distributing a musical work in physical or digital formats. A performance royalty is paid for the public performance of a work. A sync license is needed to use a musical work in film, TV, commercials, video games or YouTube videos. A master use license is needed to use a specific sound recording in a project, and the master's owner, typically a record label or an independent artist, grants it.

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams covers the same ground in its lessons: performance, mechanical, synchronization, on-demand streaming, webcasting and satellite radio.

## Start with who sent the statement

Before you read a single number, find the sender's name and the period covered. The sender tells you which kind of money you are looking at.

Streaming mechanical royalties in the United States are collected and distributed by the Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as the MLC. Under the Music Modernization Act, songwriters and music publishers must register with the MLC through its online claiming portal to receive these royalties.

Performance royalties for musical works are collected by performing rights organizations such as ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR, and songwriters must join one to collect. For sound recordings, SoundExchange administers digital performance royalties for non-interactive digital transmissions such as Pandora or satellite radio, and performing artists must register with it.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Alliance says that sound recordings have no public performance right on U.S. terrestrial radio.
:::

## Match each statement to a right

Once you know the sender, write down which right it covers. A statement from the MLC covers streaming mechanicals. A statement from a performing rights organization covers performance of the composition. A statement from SoundExchange covers digital performance of the recording. A statement from a label or distributor covers the master recording.

This step matters because the same stream can create money in more than one place. If you only register in one place, the other lines will never appear on any statement.

Title III of the Music Modernization Act also covers producers, mixers and sound engineers. It sets out a process for SoundExchange to distribute royalties to them under a letter of direction. If you worked on a recording in one of those roles, ask whether a letter of direction is in place.

## Read the line items

Statements differ by sender, and the column names vary. These are the things worth checking on any statement you receive.

- Period: the dates the money covers.
- Title and writer or artist: confirm the title, spelling and your name are right.
- Source or service: where the use happened, such as a streaming service.
- Units or streams: how many uses were counted.
- Rate or share: the part of the income that belongs to you.
- Amount: what was paid for that line.

If a line is missing, or a title is wrong, tell the sender in writing and keep a copy of the request.

## Understand how streaming pays

Spotify's Loud and Clear site explains that artists are paid based on their share of total streams on the service. If an artist's music accounts for 1 percent of all streams, they receive roughly 1 percent of the royalty pool. Spotify calls this streamshare.

This is why a line item is not a fixed amount per stream. What you are paid depends on your share of the whole pool, not on a flat price for each play.

:::note Worth knowing
Spotify describes streaming royalties as the foundation of an artist's income, and says discovery on the service can lead to touring, merchandise and sync deals.
:::

## Keep a simple record

Save every statement in one folder, named by sender and period. Once a quarter, list each sender and the total it paid. Over time you will see which services and which songs produce income, and which sender has stopped reporting.

Keeping records also helps if you ever need to ask a sender a question. A date, a title and an amount are easier to look up than a general complaint.
