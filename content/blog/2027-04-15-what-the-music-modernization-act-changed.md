If you release music, money can reach you through more than one door. Streams and downloads create royalties for the song, and separate royalties for the recording. A 2018 law changed how part of that system works. This post explains the Music Modernization Act in plain terms, one title at a time, so you know which parts could matter to you. It is general information, not legal advice.

## When it became law and what it is for

The Music Modernization Act was signed into law on October 11, 2018. The U.S. Copyright Office describes it as updating music licensing to better facilitate legal licensing of music by digital services. It also describes the law as making statutory licensing fairer for creators and more efficient for digital music providers.

The Act has three titles. Each one covers a different group of people in the music business: songwriters and publishers, owners of older recordings, and producers and engineers.

## Title I: a blanket license for songs

Title I is the Musical Works Modernization Act. It sets up a blanket licensing system that lets digital music providers make and distribute digital phonorecord deliveries. That phrase covers permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams. In practice, a streaming service can license the songs it plays through one system instead of tracking down each song's owner one at a time.

The Copyright Office designated an organization called The MLC, short for the Mechanical Licensing Collective, to collect and distribute the mechanical royalties that come from this blanket license. According to the Copyright Office, as of January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers need to register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the new blanket license.

:::note What The MLC says it does
The MLC says it administers blanket mechanical licenses for eligible streaming and download services in the United States. Each month, digital service providers send it usage data and royalties, and it matches them to registered songs before paying its members.
:::

## What a songwriter does about it

The MLC says anyone entitled to receive digital audio mechanical royalties should become a member. It describes joining as free and easy for independent songwriters, music publishers, and administrators. It also says it pays self-administered songwriters, composers, and lyricists directly, and that members can register their songs in the Member Hub within its portal.

If you wrote a song that is on streaming services, the practical question is whether your song is registered with The MLC, either by you or by a publisher or administrator who works for you. If you are not sure who is handling it, that is worth finding out before you assume the money is being collected.

## Title II: older recordings

Title II is the Classics Protection and Access Act. It partially brings sound recordings made before 1972 into the federal copyright system. It also provides federal remedies for unauthorized use of recordings fixed before February 15, 1972. The Copyright Office describes this part as creating a federal remedy and a statutory royalty structure for owners of those older recordings, with limits such as fair use.

For a working independent artist today, Title II is the part least likely to touch your own releases. It could matter if you reissue or build on older recordings, because the status of the original is part of what you need to understand first.

## Title III: producers, mixers, and engineers

Title III is the Allocation for Music Producers Act, often called the AMP Act. It lets music producers, mixers, and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. It sets a process for the designated collective, SoundExchange, to distribute those royalties under what the law calls a letter of direction.

SoundExchange says on its own site that it collects and distributes digital performance royalties on behalf of recording artists and master rights owners. If you work with a producer or engineer, agree on who is credited and what share, if any, goes to them, and put it in writing before the recording is released.

## What it means for an independent artist

Put the three titles side by side and a pattern shows. Title I is about the song, and who collects what digital services owe for using it. Title III is about the recording, and who shares in the royalties it earns. Title II is about older recordings. The song side and the recording side involve different organizations, The MLC and SoundExchange, so check each one separately.

A short checklist follows from that. Know whether your songs are registered with The MLC. Know who is listed as the owner of the master recording. Know whether your producer and engineer have agreed to any royalty share and whether it is on paper. These are questions you can ask a publisher, a distributor, a collaborator, or an attorney who works with music. For decisions about your own rights, talk with a qualified professional.
