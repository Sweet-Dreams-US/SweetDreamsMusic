If you make music, money can reach you from several directions at once. Songwriting, recording, producing and performing each create different rights, and different organizations collect for them. The Music Modernization Act changed three separate parts of that system. Knowing which part applies to you helps you register the right things and ask the right questions.

This article walks through the three titles of the Act in plain language. It is general information, not legal advice.

## What the Act is

The Orrin G. Hatch-Bob Goodlatte Music Modernization Act, usually called the MMA, updates copyright law. According to the U.S. Copyright Office, its aim is to make statutory licensing fairer to creators and more efficient for the digital music services that use their work.

It has three titles. Each one deals with a different group of people and a different kind of music right.

## Title I: songwriters and the mechanical license

Title I is the Musical Works Modernization Act. It concerns the songs themselves, meaning the composition and lyrics, rather than a particular recording of them.

Title I created a blanket license for digital uses such as permanent downloads, limited downloads and interactive streams. A digital music provider uses it by filing a notice of license with a new organization.

That organization is the Mechanical Licensing Collective, called the MLC. The Copyright Office describes it as a nonprofit that administers the blanket license from the license availability date, January 1, 2021. It receives notices and reports from providers, collects and distributes royalties, and keeps a public database of musical works and their owners.

:::note What the MLC does
The U.S. Copyright Office says the MLC collects and distributes royalties under the blanket license and maintains a public database of musical works and owners. Songwriters and publishers must register with the MLC through its claiming portal to receive those royalties.
:::

A second nonprofit, the Digital Licensee Coordinator, represents the digital services. It has a non-voting seat on the MLC board and takes part in setting the MLC's administrative assessment. The MLC's operating costs are paid by the digital providers.

When a work cannot be matched to an owner, the MLC distributes the unclaimed royalties to owners in its records, based on market share reported by the providers. That gives songwriters a strong reason to make sure their works are registered and matched.

The Act also changed how rates are set. The Copyright Office says the compulsory rates themselves are unchanged, but a new willing buyer and willing seller standard replaces the older standard in future rate proceedings.

## Title II: recordings made before 1972

Title II is the Classics Protection and Access Act. It deals with sound recordings made before February 15, 1972, which were not covered by federal copyright law before.

Under Title II, owners of these older recordings get federal remedies for unauthorized use. The protection lasts 95 years after first publication, with additional periods depending on the publication date. For some recordings the transition period runs until February 15, 2067.

No registration is required for the protection itself. To qualify for statutory damages or attorneys' fees, owners generally need to file schedules of their recordings with the Copyright Office. There is also a process for noncommercial use, which requires a notice after a good faith search for the owner, and the owner has 90 days to object.

If you work with older catalogs, reissues or archival material, Title II is the part of the Act that matters most.

## Title III: producers, mixers and engineers

Title III is the Allocation for Music Producers Act. It deals with a group that was often left out of royalty payments: the producers, mixers and engineers who helped make a recording.

The Copyright Office explains that Title III lets these contributors be paid from the statutory royalties collected under section 114, which covers certain digital performances of recordings. The designated collective, currently SoundExchange, distributes a portion of those royalties directly to them. It does so under a letter of direction from an authorized artist payee.

In practice that means a producer or engineer who wants to be paid this way needs the featured artist's authorized representative to provide a letter of direction. For some pre-1995 recordings without a letter, SoundExchange must adopt a policy for paying from the featured artist's share if the artist payee is notified and does not object.

## Putting it together for different roles

A songwriter or publisher should register their works with the MLC so royalties under the blanket license can reach them. A recording artist should know that older recordings gained federal protection under Title II, and that Title III gives producers and engineers a path to direct payment. A producer or engineer should talk with the artist about a letter of direction, preferably before the recording is released, while everyone is still in touch.

The key habit is keeping good records. Know who wrote the song, who owns the recording, who produced and mixed it, and who is authorized to sign for the artist. Those records make registrations and letters of direction much easier to complete.

## What the Act did not change

The Act is a set of specific updates, not a rewrite of everything. It did not change the compulsory rates themselves, and it did not touch private agreements such as the terms of a beat license or a contract with a label. Those remain matters between the parties.
