Streaming gets most of the attention in conversations about music income, but for many performing artists the stage is where the money is made. Understanding how live music pays, and what is left after costs, helps an artist plan their year, price their shows and decide what else to build around them.

This article covers why live music matters to artist income, how the money comes in, why the net number is the one that counts, and how video and other content can support a touring or local live career.

## Live music is a primary income source

Berklee Online, the online arm of Berklee College of Music, puts it directly in the description of its music business revenue streams course: live music "continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists." One lesson in that course is titled "The Live Music Industry: Primary Breadwinner for Performing Artists."

:::note Worth knowing
Berklee Online's course description says live music "continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists," and that artists "need to cultivate ancillary income sources" as well.
:::

That does not mean every artist earns well from live shows. It means that for a performing artist, the stage is usually a bigger piece of the income picture than recordings. The same Berklee description adds that artists need to build other income around it.

## Where the money comes from at a show

A live performance can bring in money in more than one way, and which ways apply depends on the deal. Depending on the booking, an artist may be paid by the venue or the promoter as a flat fee or as a share of ticket sales. Artists who sell items at shows, such as merchandise or recordings, can add that income to the performance fee.

Because these arrangements vary from one booking to the next, the first step is to know exactly what each show pays and under what terms before the date is confirmed. A written agreement that states the fee, the ticket split if there is one, the set length and the load-in time prevents most disputes.

## Songwriters can be paid for performances too

There is a second kind of income that comes from the same event. When a song is performed in public, the owner of the composition holds a right connected to that performance. The U.S. Copyright Office notes that composition owners can perform and display the work publicly, while sound recording owners can perform publicly only by means of a digital audio transmission.

Performing rights organizations, such as BMI, exist to turn that right into payment. BMI states that it licenses businesses to play its members' works and that the licensing fees go back to its songwriters, composers and publishers. BMI also has a Live Concert Royalties page for its members. Its public page does not explain the payment steps, so a songwriter who wants to know how live performances are reported and paid should read the current rules from their own organization.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Copyright Office says composition owners can perform and display a work publicly, while sound recording owners can perform publicly only by digital audio transmission. This is one reason songwriters and recording owners are treated differently when music is performed live.
:::

## The net is what you keep

A fee on a contract is not income. Berklee's course includes a lesson on "Compensation Issues and Getting to the 'Net'," and the idea behind that title is useful for every artist. What counts is what remains after the costs of making the show happen.

Costs commonly include travel, lodging, food, equipment, crew, and anyone who takes a share of the fee for booking or managing the artist. An artist who earns a good fee but spends most of it on getting to the show has not earned much. Before saying yes to a date, write down the likely costs and subtract them from the fee. If the result is too small, you can ask for a higher fee, ask for a ticket share, reduce the costs or turn the date down.

## Live shows work better with content around them

Berklee's advice to build other income around live music has a practical side. A live show reaches the people in the room. Video and photos can carry it to everyone else, and they give a promoter or booker something to evaluate before they book you.

Good live footage shows what your show is like, which a streaming link cannot do. Photos and short clips from a performance can be used to promote the next date, and a well-made live video can be shared long after the night is over.

## Building a live plan

A simple live plan has three parts. First, know what each type of show pays you and what it costs you, so you can compare a hometown bar, a festival slot and a private event on the same terms. Second, keep a record of every show and its songs, so that any performance income owed to you can be tracked. Third, make sure every show produces something you can use again, whether that is a video, a set of photos or a clip for social media.

Artists who treat live music as a business, with agreements, costs and content, are in a better position than those who treat each show as a one-off.
