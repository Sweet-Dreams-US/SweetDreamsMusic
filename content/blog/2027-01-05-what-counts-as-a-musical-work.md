Most people use the word "song" for everything: the tune in your head, the file on your phone, and the performance you heard last night. In the music business those are not the same thing. A song, meaning the music and the lyrics, is one work. A recording of that song is a different work. Knowing which one you are talking about helps you make better decisions when you release music.

This post explains what a musical work is, how it differs from a recording, and why that matters for artists who make and release their own music.

## The song itself

A musical work is the underlying composition. It is the melody, the harmony, the rhythm as written, and the lyrics if there are any. It exists whether or not anyone has ever recorded it. If a songwriter plays a new tune for a friend, or writes it down, the song is there. No studio is needed.

That is why the same song can be performed and recorded by many different artists. The composition stays the same while each performance is new. The musical work is the thing being performed.

## The recording is a separate work

A sound recording is the captured performance. It is the sounds fixed in a medium, such as a digital file, a disc, or a tape. According to the U.S. Copyright Office, the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics, or other content that the recording contains.

The Copyright Office gives an example in its guidance on sound recordings. The song "Rolling in the Deep" was written by Adele and Paul Epworth. A recording of Aretha Franklin performing that song is a separate work from the song. Two different works are involved, even though the listener hears one thing.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Copyright Office states that the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself and not the music, lyrics, or words embodied in it.
:::

## Who creates each work

The people behind each work can be different. The authors of a musical work are the songwriters. The authors of a sound recording are the featured performer and the producer who captured and processed the sounds, according to the Copyright Office.

This is one reason a single release can involve several people with different roles. A songwriter who never steps into the booth still created the musical work. A performer who records someone else's song created the recording, but not the song. An artist who writes, performs, and produces their own track is covering all of those roles at once.

## Why the split shows up in the law

The difference is built into how U.S. copyright law is organized. The Music Modernization Act, which the Copyright Office describes on its website, treats the two kinds of work in separate parts.

Title I of the Act deals with musical works. It replaces the older song by song compulsory licensing structure with a blanket license for digital music providers. That license covers digital phonorecord deliveries, such as permanent downloads, limited downloads, and interactive streams. The Copyright Office named Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. as the Mechanical Licensing Coordinator on July 8, 2019.

Titles II and III deal with sound recordings. Title II brings pre-1972 sound recordings partially into the federal copyright system and extends remedies for infringement to owners of sound recordings fixed before February 15, 1972. Title III allows music producers, mixers, and sound engineers to receive royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license. Those royalties are distributed through the designated collective, SoundExchange, under a letter of direction.

:::note Worth knowing
Under Title III of the Music Modernization Act, producers, mixers, and sound engineers can receive royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license, distributed through SoundExchange under a letter of direction.
:::

You do not need to memorize the titles. The point is that the law, and the organizations that handle money for music, keep songs and recordings apart.

## What this means when you release music

Before a release, it helps to ask two questions. First, who wrote the song? Second, who made the recording? Sometimes the answers are the same people, and sometimes they are not. If they are not, write down who did what while everyone remembers. Clear notes about who contributed to the song and who contributed to the recording save a lot of confusion later.

It also helps to keep the two kinds of work apart in your own files. Keep the lyrics and the written music with the song. Keep the final audio files, the stems, and the session notes with the recording. When someone asks about "the song," ask whether they mean the composition or a particular recording of it.

If you are not sure how rights apply to your own music, the Copyright Office website is a good place to read the official guidance. A music attorney can answer questions about your specific situation.

## A short summary

A musical work is the song: the music and the lyrics. A sound recording is a captured performance of that song, and it is a separate work. Songwriters are the authors of the first. The featured performer and the producer are the authors of the second. The law and the music industry treat them separately, so it pays to keep track of both when you release your music.
