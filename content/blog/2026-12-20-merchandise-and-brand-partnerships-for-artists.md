Merchandise and brand partnerships are two income sources that sit outside recordings and live shows. They are easy to misjudge. Some artists expect too much from them, and others ignore them. The research suggests that most artists earn only a small share of their income from them, but it also shows why they are worth understanding.

This post explains what merchandise and brand partnerships are, what the available numbers say, and how to decide whether either belongs in your plan.

## Where these streams sit in the music business

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams states that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. It also says artists need to cultivate ancillary income sources just to survive. Ancillary means income that comes from outside the main activity of making and performing music.

Berklee's course covers merchandise and brand partnerships as separate topics. One lesson deals with branding history, sponsorship, endorsements and brand partnerships. Another deals with artist-branded merchandise, artist-branded instruments, tribute bands and legacy brands.

Reading that list tells you something useful. These streams are built on an artist's name, image and audience. They are not built on the recordings themselves. That is why they depend on the size and loyalty of an artist's audience.

## What merchandise really means

Merchandise is any physical or digital product that carries your name, artwork or brand. Shirts, hoodies, posters, vinyl, stickers and tote bags are the usual items. The point of merchandise is not only the profit on each item. A fan who wears your shirt is also showing your name to other people.

Merchandise works best when it matches something the fan already feels. A design from a specific release, a tour, or a recognizable piece of cover art gives a fan a reason to buy. A product with only a logo on it gives much less reason.

## What the numbers say

The Future of Music Coalition ran a study of over 5,000 US-based musicians and composers called the Artist Revenue Streams project. Its first results include figures on merchandise:

- 12% of surveyed artists reported making any merchandise money in the previous 12 months.
- Merchandise made up 2% of all respondents' income.
- By genre, merchandise was 6% of income for rock artists, 5% for hip hop, 2% for country, and under 1% for jazz and classical.

The same study found that the average musician earned more from fans and grants than from merchandise and corporate sponsorships.

:::note What the study does and does not tell you
The figures above come from a survey of working musicians and are averages across all of them. They show that merchandise is a small share of income for most artists. They do not predict what one artist with an active audience will earn, and the numbers may differ from today's.
:::

For an independent artist this means merchandise is not a reliable first source of income. In this data it is a supplement to other income.

## How brand partnerships work

A brand partnership is an agreement where a company pays an artist, or gives the artist products or services, in return for the artist mentioning or using the company's product. Berklee's course groups these under sponsorship, endorsements and brand partnerships.

For a partnership to make sense, three things should line up. The brand's customers should overlap with your audience. The way the brand wants to be shown should not clash with how you present yourself. And the terms should be written down: what you will post or perform, how many times, for how long, and what you receive.

## Disclosure is a legal requirement

If you post about a product and you received money, free product or any other benefit, you must say so. The Federal Trade Commission explains that any material connection to a brand must be disclosed, and that receiving free or discounted products counts. This applies even if you think your opinion is unbiased.

The FTC also says where the disclosure should go. It should be placed with the endorsement itself, where it is hard to miss. Disclosures on a profile page, at the end of a post, or behind a "more" link are likely to be missed. In a video, the disclosure should be in the video itself, spoken or on screen, and not only in the description. The FTC suggests simple wording such as "advertisement," "ad," or "sponsored," and warns against vague terms like "sp," "spon," or "collab."

:::note A simple habit
Put the word "ad" or "sponsored" at the start of any post tied to a partnership, and say it in the video itself if you post video.
:::

## Questions to ask before you commit

Before you spend money on merchandise or sign a partnership, ask a few direct questions.

For merchandise: How many people have asked for it? What will it cost to make a small first batch? Who ships orders, and who handles returns? Does the design connect to something your fans already know?

For a brand partnership: Does the brand fit your audience? What exactly are you expected to deliver? Can you end the agreement? Is the disclosure wording agreed up front?

If you cannot answer these yet, the better use of your time is usually the release itself. A release gives you something to attach merchandise or a partner to.

## A realistic way to start

Treat merchandise and brand partnerships as extensions of the work you already do, not as replacements for it. Start small. Test one design with fans who already follow you. Keep records of what you spend and what you sell. If a brand approaches you, read the terms, check the fit, and disclose the relationship every time you post.

The research shows these streams are a minor share of income for most artists, and that live music is the main source. That is not a reason to skip them. It is a reason to plan them with realistic expectations and to build them on a real audience.
