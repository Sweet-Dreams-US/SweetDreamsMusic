Money from recorded music reaches an artist in a few different ways, and the words used for it can be confusing. An advance is not a bonus. A royalty is not a flat fee. A producer may be paid from the artist's share and not from the label's. This article explains the main terms so you can read an offer with fewer surprises.

It is general information and not legal advice. A contract is specific to the people who sign it, so have an entertainment lawyer read yours.

## What an advance is

Sound On Sound, a recording magazine, published a guide to recording contracts written by an entertainment lawyer, Richard Salmon. It describes advances as sums of money paid to the artist on account of future royalties. They can be paid when the contract is signed and again as the label exercises options for later records.

The key words are "on account of." An advance is a payment made ahead of money the recording is expected to earn. It is not extra money on top of royalties.

:::note Worth knowing
The Sound On Sound guide dates from April 2007, and its figures come from a mostly UK point of view. The percentages in this article are examples from that guide, not current industry standards. Deal terms vary, and they have changed since then.
:::

## What recoupment means

Recoupment is how the label gets its advance back. The guide says the label first recovers the advance out of the artist's royalty income. Until that happens, the artist does not receive a royalty payment.

The guide lists what an artist must recoup before royalties are paid: recording costs, advances and usually 50 percent of video costs. Tour support is also recoupable, though the author says promotion costs should not be. Advances are often paid from a recording fund that covers the costs of making the record, and any surplus goes to the artist. The full amount, though, is recoupable.

The author also advises that advances should not be repayable in cash. Otherwise an artist could personally owe money the records did not earn back. The guide says an artist should repay only to the extent that record sales cover the amount.

## How the artist royalty is figured

The guide describes the artist royalty as somewhere between 14 and 18 percent of the dealer price, and then lists deductions that reduce what the percentage is applied to. Examples include a 20 to 25 percent packaging deduction on CDs, a reduced rate on foreign and budget sales and a 10 percent reserve held back against returns.

The guide does not separate featured artists from main artists. A featured artist's share is set by the agreement that person signs, so ask for it in writing before the record is released and read how it is calculated.

## How producers are paid

On producers, the Sound On Sound guide is specific. The artist pays the producer's royalty from the artist's own share. Its example is a 3 percent producer royalty on a 15 percent artist rate, which leaves the artist 12 percent before deductions. It also says producer advances are the label's responsibility in the UK.

For an artist, the practical point is that every percentage you agree to give a producer reduces your own. For a producer, it means the royalty is tied to how the artist's deal is built.

The U.S. Copyright Office describes a newer route for producers. The Music Modernization Act includes a section called the Allocation for Music Producers Act. According to the Office, it lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected under the section 114 statutory license. SoundExchange distributes those payments under a letter of direction.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office page says only that SoundExchange makes these payments under a letter of direction and gives no further detail. If you want a producer, mixer or engineer paid this way, ask SoundExchange how it works.
:::

## Mechanical royalties belong to songwriters

A recording and the song on it are separate things. The Sound On Sound guide defines mechanical royalties as royalties paid to songwriters for compositions reproduced on sound carriers. It gives the UK rate as 8.5 percent of dealer price for physical product and 8 percent of gross revenue, excluding VAT, for downloads. For the United States, it says labels typically pay 75 percent of the statutory rate and often cap payment at ten songs per album.

The Copyright Office explains the larger change since that article was written. The Music Modernization Act has three parts. Title I replaces the song-by-song compulsory licensing structure with a blanket license for digital music providers covering downloads and interactive streams. On July 8, 2019, the Office named Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. as the Mechanical Licensing Coordinator under that title. Title II brings sound recordings from before 1972 partly into the federal system.

If you write your own songs, you can be both the artist and the songwriter, and you are paid under different parts of the system for each role.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Ask what the advance is and what it is paid against. Ask which costs are recoupable, and what share of video costs. Ask how the royalty rate is calculated and which deductions apply. Ask whether a producer's royalty comes out of your share. Ask who collects the songwriting income and how you are registered.
