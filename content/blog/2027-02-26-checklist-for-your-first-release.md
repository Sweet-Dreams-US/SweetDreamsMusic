A first release feels like a creative finish line, but it is also a set of ownership decisions. Every release involves at least two separate things that can be owned and paid separately: the song and the recording of the song. If you answer a few questions before you release, you avoid confusion later. This checklist uses guidance from the U.S. Copyright Office and Berklee Online. It is general information, not legal advice.

## 1. Know that the song and the recording are different works

The U.S. Copyright Office treats sound recordings and musical works as separate works. A sound recording is a fixed recording of a performance. A musical work is the underlying music and lyrics. Its example is a song and a recording of an artist performing that song, which are two distinct works.

Write down both. Your release has a song, and it has a recording of that song. Each one has its own owner, its own registration, and its own income.

## 2. Answer who owns the song

The musical work is the music and the lyrics. Circular 56 does not say who owns a musical work. It points readers to a different Copyright Office circular (Circular 50) for that topic. For your checklist, list everyone who wrote any part of the music or lyrics, and agree on how the song is shared among them before release, in writing.

:::note Worth knowing
The Copyright Office says the copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics or other content embodied in the recording.
:::

## 3. Answer who owns the recording

The Copyright Office says the author of a sound recording is the featured performer and the producer who captured and processed the sounds. There are exceptions. If the performer or producer made the recording as an employee, the employer is the author. If the recording was made for a third party as a contribution to a collective work, and both parties agreed in writing that it is a work made for hire, the third party is the author.

The practical point is that the arrangement with your producer, engineer and any other contributor matters, and written agreements settle questions that memory will not. Put the arrangement on paper before the release goes out.

## 4. Plan the registrations separately

The Copyright Office gives separate guidance for each kind of registration. Sound recordings are covered in Circular 56, musical compositions in Circular 50, and recordings together with their content in Circular 56A.

A sound recording application requires a completed form, a nonrefundable filing fee and a deposit. You must select "Sound Recording" as the type of work at the start, because starting with the wrong option means restarting the application. Unpublished recordings, and those published only digitally, are generally uploaded as digital files. Derivative recordings must identify the preexisting material and the new material in the application.

## 5. Register to collect song royalties

The Music Modernization Act created a blanket licensing system under which digital music providers can make and distribute downloads and interactive streams. The Copyright Office designated The Mechanical Licensing Collective, Inc. (The MLC) to collect and distribute mechanical royalties under that system.

Since January 1, 2021, songwriters and music publishers have needed to register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive royalties under the new blanket license. If you wrote your song, add this to your checklist: register your songs with The MLC.

## 6. Check how producers and engineers get paid

The Music Modernization Act also has a section for people who work on recordings. Title III, the Allocation for Music Producers Act, lets producers, mixers and sound engineers receive royalties collected for uses of sound recordings. SoundExchange distributes these payments under a "letter of direction."

If someone mixed or produced your recording, ask them whether they have or expect a letter of direction on file. Raising it before release keeps a later payment from becoming a dispute.

## 7. Understand where release income comes from

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams shows how many separate sources there are. Its outline separates publishing income, such as performance and mechanical rights of a composition, from recording income, such as the digital transmission performance right of a sound recording. It also covers sync licensing of compositions and master recordings for television, film, commercials and video games, interactive streaming revenue, and featured artist and producer advances and royalties.

The course description adds that, because of digital changes in copyright value, artists "need to cultivate ancillary income sources just to survive." Knowing which stream belongs to the song and which belongs to the recording tells you who needs to be named on each.

## The checklist in short

Before your first release, confirm these items:

1. You have named the song and the recording as two separate works.
2. Everyone who wrote the music or lyrics is listed, with agreed shares in writing.
3. You know who the author of the recording is, and any work made for hire agreement is in writing.
4. You have planned the registration for each work and chosen the right type of work.
5. Your songs are registered with The MLC.
6. Producers, mixers and engineers have confirmed how they are paid.
7. You know which income streams apply to your release.
