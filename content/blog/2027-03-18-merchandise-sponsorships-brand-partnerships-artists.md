Most artists start by thinking about one kind of income: money from the music itself. Careers are usually built on more than that. Merchandise, sponsorships and brand partnerships bring in money that does not depend on a song being played, and they can keep an artist working between releases.

This article explains what each one is, how they differ from music income, and what to think through before you pursue them.

## Why non-music income matters

Berklee Online, the online school of Berklee College of Music, describes the situation in its music business revenue streams course. The course description states that "live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists," and adds that artists "need to cultivate ancillary income sources just to survive."

:::note What ancillary means
Ancillary income is money an artist earns outside the sale or streaming of recordings. Berklee's course teaches students to design income by "extending artist brands into ancillary and non-traditional music businesses."
:::

That does not mean every artist should chase every opportunity. It means the artists who plan for more than one source of income are less exposed when any single source slows down.

## Music income and non-music income are tracked differently

Music income has its own systems, and it is worth setting them up before anything else. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that the Music Modernization Act created a blanket licensing system for digital music providers, and designated the Mechanical Licensing Collective to collect and distribute mechanical royalty payments. To receive those royalties, songwriters and publishers must sign up with the collective through its online claiming portal.

The same law also covers people who work on recordings. The Copyright Office says it allows music producers, mixers and sound engineers to receive royalties, with SoundExchange distributing the funds under a letter of direction.

None of that touches merchandise or sponsorships, which are handled through your own sales and your own agreements. Keeping the two sides separate in your records makes both easier to manage.

## Merchandise

Merchandise is anything a fan can buy that carries the artist's name or artwork. Common examples include shirts, posters, prints and physical copies of the music. Berklee's course syllabus treats merchandising as its own lesson and covers the scope of celebrity and artist branded merchandise, so it is a recognized part of how artists earn.

A few practical questions help before you order anything:

- What have fans already asked for? Requests at shows and in messages are a better guide than guessing.
- Does the item look like the artist? Artwork and photography tie the product to the music.
- How much can you afford to hold? Start with a small run of one or two items before you stock a full catalog.
- Where will you sell it? Shows, your own site and your social channels each reach different fans.

## Sponsorships and endorsements

A sponsorship is an arrangement in which a business supports an artist, a show or a project in exchange for exposure. An endorsement is when an artist publicly recommends a product. Berklee's syllabus lists sponsorship and endorsements alongside brand partnerships in its lesson on branding.

The course describes the theme of that lesson as "Coupling Parties of Like Minds, Means, and/or Needs." The idea is that the strongest arrangements join an artist and a business that share an audience or a purpose, so neither side is stretching to fit.

Before you accept any sponsorship or endorsement, consider these points:

- Does the business fit your audience? A mismatch can cost you trust with the fans you already have.
- What exactly will you provide? Posts, appearances, mentions in a video, or something else. Write the list down.
- What will you receive, and when? Payment, product, or both, with dates.
- How long does the arrangement last, and can either side end it?

Put the terms in writing. A short written agreement protects both sides, and it is worth having a lawyer review anything with money or exclusivity attached. This article is general information, not legal advice.

## Brand partnerships

A brand partnership usually goes further than a single endorsement. The artist and the business work together on something, such as a campaign, a product or a series of content. The artist's audience sees the work in their normal feed, so the content has to feel like it belongs there.

This is where good video and photography help. A business choosing a partner wants to see how the artist looks and sounds on screen. A short, well made clip and a clear set of photos make a pitch easier to understand than a written description.

## Starting without overreaching

An artist does not need all three streams at once. A reasonable order is to build the audience first, add one small piece of merchandise that fans have asked for, and look at sponsorships and partnerships once there is an audience a business would want to reach. Keep records of every sale and agreement so the income is easy to report and to compare against your costs.

Berklee's course states the goal in terms of integration: developing revenue strategies that combine live performance, branding, copyrights and recorded music income. The plan that works is one where those pieces support each other, with the music at the center and the other income built around it.
