Artists often hear that they need a manager, or that they need an agent, as if the two words meant the same thing. They do not. The two roles overlap a little at the start of a career, and they separate as the work grows. Knowing the difference helps you decide who to talk to, what to ask for, and what to have ready when you do.

This article explains what a manager does, what a booking agent does, how the two work together around live shows, and how to prepare before you approach either one.

## Why live work matters

Live performance is a large part of how many artists earn a living. Berklee Online, the online school of Berklee College of Music, says in the description of its music business revenue streams course that live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. One lesson in that course is titled "The Live Music Industry: Primary Breadwinner for Performing Artists."

That is why the people around live work matter. Shows are not only about playing well. Someone has to find the dates, speak with the venue or promoter, and make sure the artist's career fits together. Two of those people are the manager and the booking agent.

## What a manager does

A manager is the broadest role around an artist. Artist PR calls the manager the broadest role by far. The tasks can include getting gigs, some promotion, early conversations with record executives, and introductions to the rest of the artist's team.

The manager also looks at the whole path of the career. Artist PR says a manager oversees overall career strategy, and that managers often handle early bookings personally. That second point matters for new artists. At the start, there may be nobody else to call venues, so the manager does it.

Berklee Online lists two kinds of manager as separate topics in its course: the personal manager and the business manager. The course page does not describe them in detail, but it shows that the music industry treats them as different jobs.

## What a booking agent does

A booking agent has a narrower focus. Artist PR says a booking agent specializes in securing gigs and appearances. The agent's work is to find shows, speak with the people who hire artists, and arrange the dates.

According to the same source, an artist typically needs an agent once booking demand grows beyond what one person, such as a manager, can handle. At a larger scale, an artist might have different agents for different regions. Berklee Online also treats the talent agent as its own role, with a lesson on the role and the revenue streams that go with it.

## How the two work together

The simplest way to see the difference is by the question each one answers. The manager asks where the artist's career is going and what should happen next. The booking agent asks which shows fit and which dates can be filled.

In practice, the manager sets direction and holds the pieces together, and the agent fills the calendar. When the artist is small, one person may do both. As the calendar fills up, the work usually splits, because finding shows takes steady attention that a manager also needs for everything else.

If you are an artist with a few shows a month and no team, you are probably your own manager and your own booking agent. That is normal. The question is when the work grows beyond what you can handle alone.

## When to look for help

There is no single date when an artist needs a manager or an agent. A few signs point toward it. Offers come in faster than you can answer them. You are spending more time on emails and calls than on music. You are turning down shows because you cannot manage the schedule. Or you have a clear plan for a bigger step, such as a tour, and no one to organize it.

Because a booking agent is needed once demand outgrows a single person, many artists begin with a manager or handle the early shows themselves, and add an agent when the calendar demands it. That order is not a rule, but it matches what Artist PR describes.

Before you agree to work with anyone, ask for the terms in writing and read them. Ask what the person will do, what they will be paid, and how long the agreement lasts. Take a contract to a lawyer who works with musicians if you can.

## What to have ready

Whoever you approach, they will want to see your work quickly. Having material ready makes it easier for a manager or an agent to understand what you do and to pass it on to a venue or a promoter.

Useful things to have include a clear photo, a short video of you playing live, current links to your music, and a simple page that shows your upcoming dates and how to reach you. Good live footage is especially helpful for booking, because a venue wants to see how you sound and move in a room.

## A short summary

A manager has the broadest role. They oversee the whole career, and they often handle early bookings. A booking agent focuses on getting gigs, and is typically needed once demand grows beyond one person. The two work together, and early on you may fill both roles yourself. Know the difference, get your terms in writing, and have good photos, video, and links ready before you reach out.
