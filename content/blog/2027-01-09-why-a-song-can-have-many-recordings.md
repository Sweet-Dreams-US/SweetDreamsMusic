You have probably heard the same song performed by two different artists. A singer covers a hit from another decade, a band records its own version of a folk tune, or a producer releases a new take on a well known track. It is natural to ask whether these are the same thing or two different things.

The answer shapes how artists get paid, who owns what, and what must be cleared before a release. It starts with one idea: a song and a recording of that song are two separate works.

## The song and the recording are different works

The U.S. Copyright Office separates the two in its guidance on sound recordings. A sound recording is, in general, a recorded performance, often of another work. The copyright in a sound recording covers the recording itself. It does not cover the music, lyrics, or other content that the recording captures.

The music and lyrics are a separate work, which the Office calls a musical work. In everyday terms, the musical work is the song as written. The sound recording is one particular performance of it that was captured and processed into a file or a record.

## One song, many recordings

Because the two are separate, one song can be recorded again and again. Each new recording is its own sound recording, made by its own performers and producers, while the song underneath stays the same musical work.

The Copyright Office gives a clear example in its circular. It describes a song written by Adele and Paul Epworth and a recording of that song by Aretha Franklin, and it states that these are two distinct works. A new recording of an existing song is a separate sound recording, and the song remains a separate musical work.

## Who is behind each one

The two works usually have different people behind them. A song is created by its writers, which can be a songwriter, a composer, a lyricist, or a group of them. A recording is created by the performer featured on it and the producer who captured and processed the sounds.

The Copyright Office explains that the author of a sound recording is the featured performer and the producer. If those people made the recording as employees, the employer is the author as a work made for hire. If they made it for someone else under a written work made for hire agreement, that other party is the author.

So the people who hold the song and the people who hold the recording are often not the same. A record company, a producer, or a company owned by the performer may hold the recording, while the writers hold the song.

## When a recording is not a new recording

Not every change to a recording makes a new one. The Copyright Office notes that a recording built from preexisting sounds may be a derivative sound recording, but only if it adds new original sound recording authorship. Mechanical changes, such as converting to another format or reducing noise, generally do not add enough original authorship.

For an artist, the practical meaning is that cleaning up an old file does not turn it into a new recording. Recording the song again, with a new performance, does. It also means a cover version needs its own credits, because the performers and producers of that recording are different from those on the original.

## Why this matters when you release music

If you record a song that someone else wrote, you are making a new sound recording of an existing musical work. You will own or control the recording, depending on your agreements with the people who helped make it. The song still belongs to its writers, and its use is handled through separate licensing channels.

The Copyright Office also describes how the Music Modernization Act changed part of this. Its Title I replaces the older song by song licensing structure with a blanket license for digital downloads and interactive streams provided by digital music services. Titles II and III deal with sound recordings, including a share of certain royalties for producers and engineers.

These are general descriptions of how the system is set up, not advice on a particular release. If you plan to release a recording of a song you did not write, or you are unsure who owns your own recordings, talk with a music attorney before you publish.

## What to take from this

- A song and a recording of it are two works with their own owners.
- One song can have any number of recordings.
- A new performance makes a new recording. Technical cleanup of an old one generally does not.
- Writers, performers, and producers can each have a different claim, so write down who did what and who owns what at the start of a project.
