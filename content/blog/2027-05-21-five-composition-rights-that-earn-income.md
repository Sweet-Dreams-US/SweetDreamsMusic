Most artists think of a song as one thing. In music business terms it is two. There is the composition, which is the melody and the lyrics, and there is the sound recording, which is one particular recorded performance of that composition. Each one is its own piece of property, and each one earns money in its own ways.

This post is about the composition. The person or people who wrote it hold a bundle of rights under copyright law, and each right can pay in a different way, from a different source. Five of them come up again and again: the performance right, the mechanical right, the derivative right, the print right and the grand right. Knowing which is which helps you check that the money you are owed has a way to reach you.

## Why the composition and the recording are separate

When a song streams, two sets of owners can be paid. The owner of the recording is paid for the recording. The writers and publishers are paid for the composition. The U.S. Copyright Office makes the same point about mechanical licenses: the license covers the musical work and does not cover sound recordings, which are licensed separately.

That split is why a writer who never owned a master recording can still earn income from a song. It is also why an artist who owns every recording they make can still miss composition income if the song was never registered anywhere. The five rights below all belong to the composition side.

## The performance right

The performance right covers playing a song in public. The Copyright Alliance lists concerts, venues, bars, restaurants, television and digital streaming platforms as examples. For musical works, these royalties are collected by performing rights organizations, often called PROs. The U.S. names are ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR.

The Copyright Office guide says you must sign an agreement with a PRO to be paid, and that SESAC and GMR are invitation-only. It also says copyright owners can still license performances directly. For most new writers the practical step is the same: join one PRO and register your songs with it.

:::note Worth knowing
PROs license performance rights on a non-exclusive basis and typically pay songwriters and publishers directly, according to the U.S. Copyright Office.
:::

## The mechanical right

The mechanical right covers reproducing and distributing the composition. That includes vinyl and CDs, and it includes downloads and interactive streams. In the U.S. this works through a compulsory license, which means that once a song has been released with the owner's permission, a qualifying user can generally get a license without the owner's consent.

For digital uses, the Music Modernization Act set up a blanket license and the Copyright Office designated The Mechanical Licensing Collective, known as The MLC, to collect and distribute those royalties. The Copyright Office states that songwriters and publishers must register with The MLC through its online claiming portal to receive them. For physical uses such as vinyl and CDs, the guide says licensing agents such as the Harry Fox Agency or Music Reports often handle clearance.

Registering is not automatic. If your songs and ownership shares are not in the system, there is nobody for the money to go to.

## The derivative right

Copyright gives the owner the exclusive right to prepare derivative works. The Copyright Office guide gives interpolations, sampling, remixes and synchronization as examples. Synchronization, usually called sync, means pairing a song with audiovisual content such as films, television, commercials and video games.

There is no compulsory license for sync. The Copyright Office says copyright owners or their representatives negotiate these licenses directly, and that publishers commonly handle this for songwriters. That means the fee and the terms are whatever the two sides agree to, and nothing makes the owner say yes.

## The print right

The print right covers printed music, such as sheet music. The Copyright Office guide mentions it only briefly, in connection with publishers licensing sheet music and sub-publishing. The Berklee Online course outline treats it as its own topic and describes a shift from paper to digital distribution.

The sources give little detail on how print income is paid. If you are signed to a publisher, ask how print licensing is handled in your agreement. If you are not, there may be nothing to collect yet.

## Grand rights

Grand rights, also called dramatic performance rights, cover theatrical uses of music such as ballets, musicals and operas. Songtrust describes a dramatic performance as one that uses the work to tell a story or move a plot forward. Songs placed in a story count whether or not there is staging.

This right sits outside the PRO system. Songtrust states that ASCAP and BMI do not issue grand rights licenses, and New Music USA says grand rights fall outside the blanket licenses PROs offer. The license is negotiated for each production between the writer or publisher and the producer. New Music USA notes that fees are often a share of box office receipts.

:::note Worth knowing
Songtrust states that a concert version of a dramatic-musical work, performed without costumes or sets, can still fall under grand rights.
:::

If a song of yours is ever requested for a stage show or a dance piece, the license is something you or your publisher negotiate, and the PRO will not do it for you.

## Putting it to use

You do not need to master all five today. A short checklist covers most of the early work.

First, register each song with a PRO so performance royalties have somewhere to go. Second, register your songs and ownership shares with The MLC so digital mechanical royalties can be paid. Third, keep a written record of who wrote what and what share each person owns. Fourth, get any use of your song in someone else's video or production agreed in writing.
