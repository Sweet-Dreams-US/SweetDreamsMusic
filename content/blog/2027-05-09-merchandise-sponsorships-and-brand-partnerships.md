Streams are only one way an artist earns money. Merchandise, sponsorships, and brand partnerships are three others. They get a lot of attention, and they are often described as easy extras. The research says it is more mixed than that.

This article explains what each one is, what the available evidence says about how much it contributes, and what an artist can do to prepare.

## Why artists look beyond streams

Berklee Online's course on music business revenue streams says live music continues to provide the bulk of revenue for musical artists. It also says artists need to cultivate ancillary income sources just to survive. Merchandise and branding are two of the areas the course covers, including sponsorship, endorsements, and brand partnerships.

The course description does not give revenue figures. It makes the case that artists benefit from extending their brand into other kinds of income.

## Merchandise

Merchandise means products that carry an artist's name, image, or artwork. T-shirts are the familiar example, but the category is wider. Berklee's course covers merchandising beyond the tour and the expansion of an artist's brand.

What does the data show? The Future of Music Coalition ran a large survey of musicians and composers in the United States and released early results in 2012. Reporting on the study says more than 5,000 respondents took part. Only 12 percent of surveyed artists reported earning money from merchandise in the previous 12 months, and merchandise made up 2 percent of all respondents' income. By genre, merchandise was 6 percent of income for rock musicians, 5 percent for hip hop, and 2 percent for country, and under 1 percent for jazz and classical.

:::note Worth knowing
The Future of Music Coalition data is from 2012, so treat it as a picture of how things looked then, not now. It does show that merchandise income was uneven across artists and genres.
:::

More recent research looks at the buyer. MIDiA Research surveyed 1,000 US fans who had bought merchandise, physical music, or concert tickets in the past year, and published results in March 2026. Some of its findings for artists:

Fans aged 16 to 24 buy fewer t-shirts than other age groups but more hoodies, posters, keychains, and jewelry. They also prefer subtle branding over obvious artist logos.

Just under half of respondents say they mainly buy merch from larger, well-known artists, and that preference weakens with each younger age group.

In hip hop and R&B, more than half of merch, physical music, and ticket buyers count as fans, and those fans spend above average.

Just under half of respondents say merch is becoming unaffordable, and 39 percent say they sometimes feel their fandom is being exploited.

The article's advice is to nurture fandom rather than rush to monetize it.

## Sponsorships

A sponsorship is an arrangement where a company supports an artist, a tour, a release, or a series of content in exchange for visibility or association. Berklee's course lists sponsorship alongside endorsements and brand partnerships as part of artist branding.

The sources used for this article do not give figures for sponsorship income. The reporting on the Future of Music Coalition study says the average musician gets more from fan funding and grants than from merchandise and corporate sponsorships. That is a reminder that sponsorship is not a given for most artists.

## Brand partnerships

A brand partnership is broader. It can mean a product collaboration, a campaign, or a long-term pairing between an artist and a company. Berklee's course titles its branding lesson as coupling parties of like minds, means, or needs. In plain terms, both sides have to get something from the deal.

The course teaches branding through case studies of established artists. Those are famous examples, and a newer artist's situation will look different. What carries over is the idea that a partnership works when the artist and the brand share an audience or a point of view.

## What an artist can prepare

None of the three sources promises that merch, sponsorships, or partnerships will pay. A few practical points follow from what they say.

Start with the fans. The MIDiA findings suggest that what fans want from merch is specific to the artist and the fan base, and that younger fans respond to subtle branding. Knowing your audience comes before choosing products.

Keep your visual material ready. A sponsor, a brand, or a merch page all need clear photos and video of the artist. Having current press photos, cover art, and short video clips ready makes it easier to show what you do.

Be realistic. The 2012 data shows that merch was a small part of most artists' income then. The 2026 survey shows demand exists, but fans are watching price and feel the pressure of constant selling.

## The short version

Merchandise, sponsorships, and brand partnerships are ways to earn beyond streams, but the evidence does not show them as a sure thing. Older data shows merch was a small share of income for most artists, and newer data shows fans who want something specific and are sensitive to price. The artists best placed to use these streams know their audience, keep their visuals current, and plan before they sell.
