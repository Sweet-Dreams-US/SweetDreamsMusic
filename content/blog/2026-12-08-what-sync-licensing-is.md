If a song plays in a film, a commercial, a video game or a brand's YouTube video, someone had to give permission for that to happen. The permission is called a sync license, and it is one of the ways music earns money outside of streams and shows. It is also one of the parts of the music business that new artists tend to learn about late.

This article explains what sync licensing is, why a single song needs more than one license, what a deal usually covers and what an independent artist can do to be ready.

## What "sync" means

Sync is short for synchronization. It describes pairing music with moving images, so that the song is timed to the picture. Promise Legal, a law firm that writes about music rights for independent artists, defines a sync license as "the legal permission to pair your recorded music with visual media." RouteNote, a music distributor, lists the same kinds of uses: YouTube videos, wedding videos, and film and game soundtracks.

Berklee Online treats sync as its own revenue stream. Its course on music business revenue streams includes a lesson on generating revenue from the synchronization of compositions and the use of master recordings in TV, film, commercials and video games.

## One song, two copyrights

The reason a sync deal usually needs two licenses is that a recorded song contains two separate copyrights. The U.S. Copyright Office explains that a musical composition and a sound recording are different works. The composition is the melody, rhythm, harmony and any lyrics. The sound recording is a particular performance, fixed as a series of sounds.

:::note Worth knowing
The U.S. Copyright Office says that copyright in a sound recording is "not the same as, or a substitute for, copyright in the underlying musical composition." The two are registered separately in most cases.
:::

Each copyright has its own license in a sync deal. The sync license covers the composition. According to Promise Legal, the songwriter or the songwriter's music publisher grants it. The master use license covers one specific recording. The owner of the recording grants it, which is typically a record label or, for an independent artist, the artist.

A person who writes and records their own song, and who has not signed away either right, may hold both. That is the main reason independent artists can sometimes say yes to a licensing request quickly. A song with a co-writer, a publisher or a label attached needs each of those parties to agree.

## What a sync deal covers

There is no standard price for sync. Promise Legal states that the fee is fully negotiated, with no statutory rates, and it lists four terms that shape a deal.

Term is how long the licensee can use the music. A license with no end date is worth more than one that lasts a year. Territory is where the finished video can be shown, and a worldwide license is worth more than one limited to North America. Media is which platforms and formats are covered, and an "all media" clause is the broadest. Fee is the amount paid, and it depends on the other three.

:::note Worth knowing
Promise Legal gives example fee ranges of $1,000 to $15,000 for independent film and $10,000 to $250,000 or more for a national commercial of 30 to 60 seconds. These are examples from one law firm's guide, not fixed rates.
:::

The wide range tells you the same thing the article does. Sync fees depend on who is asking, how the song will be used, and what rights they want in return.

## What sync is not

Sync is often confused with other licenses. A mechanical license covers the composition and is needed to release a cover version. RouteNote notes that a cover cannot use the original audio, so a cover needs a new recording. The Music Modernization Act changed how mechanical licensing works for digital music providers. The U.S. Copyright Office describes its first title as replacing the song-by-song compulsory licensing structure with a blanket license for digital providers covering permanent downloads, limited downloads and interactive streams. That law is about those digital uses. The Copyright Office's summary does not mention sync.

This matters because a blanket license does not cover a video. If a song is going into a film, an ad or a brand video, the sync and master use permissions still have to be negotiated.

## How artists can get ready

Preparation is mostly record keeping. Know who wrote each song and what share each person owns. Know who owns each master recording. Keep the names, splits and any signed agreements in one place, so you can answer a licensing request without searching for them.

Berklee's course description also says artists need to cultivate ancillary income sources, meaning income beyond one main stream. Sync can be one of them, but it is not a plan you can count on for any single song.

## Questions to ask before saying yes

When a request arrives, ask what the video is, where it will run, for how long and in which countries. Ask whether the use is for one platform or for all media. Ask whether the project needs just the song or also the recording. Get the terms in writing, with the fee, before the music is used.
